A test that is right 99% of the time

It comes back positive. The chance you have the disease is nine percent. Nothing about the test is broken — the missing ingredient is how common the disease was before anyone tested, and forgetting it is the most expensive habit in applied probability.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Module 05 showed how weak the evidence in a small sample is. This module is about what to do with weak evidence: combine it with what you already believed, in the only way that is arithmetically consistent. That is all Bayes' rule is.

  1. Chapter 01 is the classic, and it is worth being surprised by. A 99%-accurate test on a 1-in-1000 disease gives a 9% chance of being sick when it comes back positive.
  2. Chapter 02 draws the rule as a rectangle, because the formula hides what the picture makes obvious: you are comparing two areas, and the base rate sets how big they start.
  3. Chapters 04 and 05 are the trading versions. A 6% day flips your view of a stock's volatility from 90% confident to 0.4% in one observation — and a good backtest on a strategy you had no prior reason to believe leaves it under a third likely to be real.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
PriorWhat you believed before the evidence. Usually a base rate, and usually the thing people skip.
LikelihoodHow probable the evidence is under each hypothesis. Not the probability of the hypothesis.
PosteriorWhat you believe after. The output of the rule.
Base rateHow common something is in the population before you looked at this case.
SensitivityThe chance a test catches a true case. P(positive | sick).
SpecificityThe chance a test clears a healthy one. P(negative | not sick).
False positiveA positive result on a case that is not real. On a rare condition these outnumber the true ones.
Likelihood ratioOne hypothesis's likelihood divided by another's. How much the evidence moves you, independent of what you believed.
01

The test is right 99% of the time and you are probably fine

Both statements are true at once. Reconciling them is the whole module.

A disease affects one person in a thousand. A test catches 99% of those who have it and correctly clears 99% of those who do not. You test positive. What is the chance you are sick?

Most people say 99%, some say 95%, and the answer is 9.02%.

Count it out on a hundred thousand people. 100 of them are sick, and the test flags 99 of those. The other 99,900 are healthy, and the test wrongly flags 1% of them — 999 people. So there are 1,098 positive results and only 99 of them are real: 99 / 1,098 = 9.02%.

Nothing is wrong with the test. The problem is that the healthy group is a thousand times larger, so even a 1% error rate applied to it produces ten times more false positives than there are true cases to find. When a condition is rare, most positives are false, and that is arithmetic rather than a criticism.

Try this

Raise the prevalence to 10% and predict the answer before you look. Then drop the test's specificity to 95% at 0.1% prevalence and see which lever matters more.

Check yourself

Which change helps more on a rare condition: raising sensitivity from 99% to 99.9%, or raising specificity from 99% to 99.9%?

Specificity, and it is not close. Sensitivity can only recover the one true case in a hundred you were missing; specificity removes nine hundred of the false ones. On rare conditions the false positive rate is the entire ballgame, which is why screening programs are judged on it and why "the test is 99% accurate" is a sentence that hides the number that matters.
02

The rule, drawn as a rectangle

Bayes' theorem is a formula for comparing two areas. The picture is easier to hold than the algebra and harder to get backwards.

Draw a square for the whole population. Split it left-to-right by whether the thing is true — the left slice is the prior. Then split each side top-to-bottom by whether the test came back positive.

You now have four boxes. A positive result tells you that you are in one of the two top boxes and nothing more. The answer is simply the true-positive box divided by the two of them together.

P(sick | positive) = P(positive | sick)·P(sick) / P(positive)

Reads as: the true-positive area divided by the total positive area. Everything in the formula is a box in the rectangle, which is why the picture is worth learning first.

The most useful thing the picture shows is what happens when you drag the prior. At a 50% base rate the two columns are equal and a 99% test gives a 99% answer, exactly as intuition demands. Slide the prior to 1% and the right-hand column becomes 99 times wider, so its 1% error stripe outgrows the left column's entire true-positive box. The test never changed.

There is a shortcut worth carrying. The evidence's strength is the likelihood ratio — here 99 to 1 — and it multiplies your odds, not your probability. Prior odds of 1:999, times 99, gives posterior odds of 99:999, which is 9.02%. Working in odds turns Bayes into a single multiplication.

Check yourself

Which quantity does the likelihood ratio multiply?

The odds, which is why odds are the natural currency of evidence. In odds form Bayes is a multiplication and nothing else, and independent pieces of evidence just multiply in sequence. It is also why module 01's notations were worth learning — betting odds and evidential odds are the same object.
03

The second test does what the first could not

9% to 91% on one more look, because the prior has moved and the evidence has not.

You test positive and the answer is 9.02%. Now take the test again, independently, and it comes back positive a second time.

The rule handles this without any new machinery: yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior. Starting from 9.02% rather than 0.1%, the same 99-to-1 likelihood ratio takes you to 90.75%. A third positive gets you to 99.90%.

This is the answer to the most common objection to chapter 01 — that a 9% result makes the test sound useless. It is not useless; it moved you from 0.1% to 9%, which is a ninety-fold increase in your belief. The problem is only that ninety times a very small number is still a small number, and the fix is more evidence rather than a better test.

It is also why screening protocols are built as sequences. The first test is cheap and casts a wide net; the second is expensive and only gets run on a population whose base rate the first test has already raised by two orders of magnitude.

Check yourself

Two positive tests take you from 0.1% to 90.75%. What does that say about the first result?

Informative, from a bad starting point. The third answer is the right thing to worry about in practice — repeat tests on the same person often share a failure mode, and if they do, the second positive is worth far less than the arithmetic here assumes. Independence is the load-bearing assumption in every sequential update.
04

Was that a four-sigma day, or was your volatility wrong?

The trading version, and the update is violent: one observation takes a 90% belief to under one percent.

You believe a stock's volatility is 20%, with maybe a 10% chance it is really 40%. Then it moves 6% in a day.

Under the 20% story a 6% day is a 4.76-sigma event — the daily standard deviation is 1.26%, so 6% is nearly five of them. Under the 40% story it is 2.38 sigma, unusual but ordinary. The likelihoods are 0.0004 and 0.9297, a ratio of about 2,470 to 1 in favor of high volatility.

Multiply that into prior odds of 9 to 1 and the posterior is 0.36% for the low-volatility story. One day has taken you from 90% confident to essentially certain you were wrong.

That is the correct response and it feels wrong, which is the lesson. Extreme observations are much more informative than ordinary ones, precisely because they are so unlikely under one hypothesis and unremarkable under another. The instinct to call a 6% day "an outlier" and carry on is the instinct to throw away the most informative data point you have.

The honest caveat: this calculation assumed the only two possibilities were 20% and 40%. Real hypothesis spaces are wider, and a genuine third option — "the volatility is 20% but the return distribution has fat tails" — would absorb some of that update. Module 04 is exactly that hypothesis, and distinguishing it from a volatility change takes more than one day.

Try this

Find the size of move that leaves you exactly 50/50. It is smaller than most people expect.

Check yourself

Why does a single extreme day move your belief so much more than a hundred ordinary ones?

Discrimination, not recency. Evidence is only worth what it can distinguish between. A day inside one sigma has a likelihood ratio close to one and therefore tells you almost nothing, however many of them you collect — which is why volatility estimates barely move through calm periods and lurch during crises.
05

Your backtest looked great. Now what?

The base rate for trading strategies is brutal, and it is the number nobody puts in the deck.

You test a strategy and the backtest is good. What is the chance the edge is real?

You cannot answer without three numbers, and only one of them is on the page in front of you. How often is a tested idea genuinely profitable? Optimistically, one in twenty. How often does a real edge produce a good backtest? Call it 80%. How often does a dead strategy produce a good backtest anyway? With enough parameters and enough attempts, at least 10%.

Run those through: 0.05 × 0.80 = 0.04 of real strategies pass, and 0.95 × 0.10 = 0.095 of dead ones do. So P(real | good backtest) = 0.04 / 0.135 = 29.6%.

A good backtest on a strategy you had no prior reason to believe leaves it more likely dead than alive. And the number is fragile in the wrong direction: it is the prior that dominates. At a 1% base rate a good backtest gets you to 7.5%; at 20% it gets you to 67%.

Which is why "I had a reason to look here" is not a soft consideration — it is the largest term in the calculation. A strategy motivated by a structural argument starts from a higher prior and needs far less evidence to become believable than one found by search.

Check yourself

Two strategies produce identical backtests. One came from a structural argument about market microstructure; the other from searching ten thousand parameter combinations. Are they equally credible?

Both terms move, and they compound. The searched strategy starts from a lower prior and has a far higher chance of producing a good backtest while being dead — ten thousand attempts at a 10% false-positive rate produces a thousand good-looking dead strategies. That is not a reason never to search; it is a reason to hold searched results to enormously more evidence than reasoned ones.

Question 1 · chapter 01

A disease affects 1 in 1,000. A test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific. You test positive. What is the chance you are sick?

Nine percent. The healthy population is a thousand times larger, so its 1% error rate produces ten times more positives than the sick population's 99% hit rate does. On rare conditions, most positives are false — and no improvement in sensitivity fixes that.
Question 2 · chapter 01

Which improvement helps more on a rare condition?

Specificity, by a mile. Sensitivity can recover at most the one true case you were missing; specificity removes nine hundred false ones. This is the whole design principle behind screening: cast the wide net first, then confirm with something specific.
Question 3 · chapter 02

Bayes' rule in odds form multiplies your prior odds by what?

The likelihood ratio, and it is the cleanest way to think about evidence. It separates cleanly into "what I believed" and "how much this observation discriminates," and independent observations just multiply their ratios together.
Question 4 · chapter 03

After two independent positive tests, the 9.02% becomes what?

Over ninety percent, from the same test twice. Which shows the first result was informative all along — it multiplied the odds by 99. The catch in practice is independence: two runs of the same test on the same sample often fail the same way, and then the second result is worth far less.
Question 5 · chapter 04

You are 90% sure a stock's volatility is 20% rather than 40%. It moves 6% in a day. Now what?

Under half a percent, from one day. Extreme observations carry enormous likelihood ratios precisely because they are so improbable under one hypothesis. Calling a 6% day "an outlier" and dropping it discards the single most informative data point available.
Question 6 · chapter 05

One in twenty tested strategies is real; good strategies backtest well 80% of the time and dead ones 10%. Your strategy backtests well. Chance it is real?

Under a third. And the term doing the work is the prior: at a 1% base rate the same backtest gets you to 7.5%, at 20% it gets you to 67%. Which is why the reason you looked matters more than the result you found.

Every sequential update on this page assumes the pieces of evidence are independent given the hypothesis, and that assumption is usually the weakest link. Two runs of the same medical test on the same sample share failure modes; two backtests on overlapping data share the same lucky period; two days of returns during the same crisis are not two independent observations of volatility. Where the independence fails, these calculations overstate how much you have learned, sometimes enormously. Chapter 04 additionally restricts the hypothesis space to exactly two volatilities, which makes the update sharper than an honest continuous prior would — and it excludes the third explanation, that volatility is unchanged and the return distribution has fat tails, which module 04 argues is often the better story. Chapter 05's numbers are illustrative rather than measured: nobody knows the true base rate for trading strategies, and the point of the chapter is that the answer depends on it far more than on the backtest.
