What a sample is allowed to tell you

You have a year of returns and want two numbers out of it. One of them you can have to a decimal place; the other you cannot have at all, and no amount of data will fix it. Knowing which is which is most of applied statistics.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Every number computed from data is an estimate with a size of error attached, and the error is usually invisible on the screen that reports it. This module is about attaching it — and about the striking asymmetry between two quantities that come out of exactly the same column of numbers.

  1. Chapters 02 and 03 are the pair to remember. Five years of daily returns pins a stock's average return to within ±28 percentage points — useless — and its volatility to within ±0.8. Same data, same column, opposite verdicts.
  2. Chapter 04 is the tool. The bootstrap gets a confidence interval by resampling instead of by algebra, works when no formula exists, and agrees with the formula when one does.
  3. Chapter 05 is the sentence everyone says wrong. A 95% interval does not mean a 95% chance the truth is inside it.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
EstimatorA recipe for turning data into a number. The sample mean is an estimator of the true mean.
Standard errorThe standard deviation of an estimator across repeated samples. How much the number would wobble if you did it again.
σ / √nThe standard error of a sample mean. The most-used formula in statistics.
σ / √(2n)The standard error of a sample standard deviation. Note the 2 — it is why volatility is easier to measure than drift.
Confidence intervalA range built by a procedure that captures the truth a stated fraction of the time. Not a probability about this particular range.
BootstrapResampling your own data with replacement to see how much a statistic wobbles, without deriving anything.
BiasA systematic error that does not shrink with more data. Different from noise, and worse.
n − 1The divisor in the sample variance, which corrects a bias you can watch appear.
01

Every number from data is really two numbers

The estimate, and how much it would move if you collected the data again. Reporting the first without the second is the default and it is a mistake.

Compute the average of a hundred numbers and you get an answer. Collect a different hundred from the same source and you get a different answer. The spread of those answers is the standard error, and it is the second number that should always travel with the first.

For a mean it is σ/√n. That formula does two things worth separating. It says more data helps — and it says more data helps slowly, because of the square root. Four times the data halves the error. A hundred times the data divides it by ten.

The figure below draws the estimate from repeated samples so the standard error is something you can see rather than compute. Take one sample and you get a number; take a thousand and you get the distribution that number was drawn from.

standard error of the mean = σ / √n

Reads as: the wobble in your average is the wobble in the data divided by the square root of how much you collected. Quadruple the data to halve the error.

Check yourself

You want to halve the standard error of an estimate. How much more data do you need?

Four times. Which sets the economics of every data collection decision: the first hundred observations buy far more precision than the second hundred, and getting from "roughly right" to "precise" is usually not worth what it costs. The fourth answer is a real consideration for the size of the error but not for how it scales.
02

A stock's average return cannot be measured

Not "is hard to measure." Five years of daily data gives an annualized mean with a 95% range 55 points wide.

Take a stock whose daily returns have a standard deviation of 2% — an ordinary single name. You want its expected return. Compute the sample mean and attach the standard error.

One month of data, twenty observations: the standard error of the daily mean is 0.447%, which annualizes to 113%. A full year, 252 observations: 31.8%. Five years, 1,260 observations: 14.2%, so the 95% interval is roughly ±28 percentage points.

Read that last one carefully. After five years of daily data, a stock whose true annual drift is 8% has a measured drift consistent with anything from −20% to +36%. The estimate is not merely imprecise — it carries essentially no information about the quantity you asked for.

This is not a flaw in the method. It is the ratio of signal to noise in financial returns: the drift is a fraction of a percent a day and the noise is two percent a day, so the noise is several hundred times larger. No estimator can extract what is not there, and every claim about expected returns built on a few years of history is weaker than it sounds.

Try this

Find the number of years at which the interval finally becomes narrower than the drift itself. Predict first whether it is closer to five years or fifty.

Check yourself

A fund reports a 12% annualized return over three years. What does that tell you about its expected return?

Very little, and the fourth answer overcorrects. Three years is real evidence; it is just much weaker evidence than the headline suggests, and it should be combined with a prior about how common skill is — which is module 10. The right response to a short track record is neither belief nor dismissal but a small update.
03

Volatility, from the same data, is measurable

One year pins it to within ±0.9 points. Same column of numbers, completely different answer.

Now ask the same data for the volatility instead. The standard error of a standard deviation is approximately σ/√(2n) — note the 2, which comes from the fact that you are estimating a second moment and there are twice as many degrees of freedom in it.

For a stock at 20% volatility: 20 days of data gives a standard error of 3.16 volatility points, so a 95% range of 13.8% to 26.2% — wide. 252 days gives 0.89 points, a range of 18.25% to 21.75%. That is a genuinely useful number, from a single year of data.

Turn it around: to pin a 20% volatility to within ±1 point at 95% confidence you need about 769 trading days, roughly three years. To within ±2 points, 193 days.

So the same year of returns that tells you nothing about drift tells you volatility to better than a point. That asymmetry is the reason options desks trade volatility rather than direction: one of those quantities can be estimated, hedged and checked, and the other cannot.

se(mean) ≈ σ/√n se(σ̂) ≈ σ/√(2n)

Reads as: both errors fall like 1/√n, but they are measuring quantities of wildly different size relative to the noise. Drift is a fraction of a percent against 2% of noise; volatility is 20% against its own scale.

Check yourself

Why can a year of data pin down volatility but not drift, when both come from the same returns?

Signal to noise, and the first answer is a separate true thing. Volatility does cluster and persist, which helps forecasting — but even for a perfectly constant process the estimation problem is easier, because you are asking about the size of the noise rather than about a tiny drift buried inside it.
04

The bootstrap: resample instead of deriving

Draw from your own data with replacement, recompute, repeat. It works for statistics that have no formula, and matches the formula where one exists.

Every standard error above came from algebra. For a mean or a standard deviation that algebra exists; for a median, a ratio, a maximum drawdown or a Sharpe ratio it either does not or is unpleasant. The bootstrap sidesteps all of it.

The recipe: take your n observations, draw n of them with replacement to make a new sample, compute your statistic, and repeat a few thousand times. The spread of the results is the standard error. That is the entire method.

The reason it works is that your sample is the best available stand-in for the population, so resampling from it mimics collecting fresh data. It is not magic and it is not free — the bootstrap can only tell you about variation your sample already contains, so it understates tail uncertainty in exactly the situations where you most want it.

Test it against a case where the formula is known. One year of daily returns with an observed annualized volatility of 21.81%: the bootstrap gives a 95% interval of 19.61% to 23.89%, and the σ/√(2n) formula gives 19.90% to 23.71%. Close enough that the choice does not matter — which is the point of checking.

Try this

Switch the statistic to the maximum drawdown, which has no simple formula at all, and watch the bootstrap produce an interval anyway.

Check yourself

What is the bootstrap's main limitation?

It cannot invent what it has not seen. If your year of data contains no crash, no amount of resampling will produce one, and the bootstrap interval for a tail statistic will be confidently narrow and wrong. It is an excellent tool for the middle of a distribution and a dangerous one for the edges — the same warning that runs through module 07.
05

What a confidence interval actually claims

Not "95% chance the truth is in here." The 95% is a property of the procedure, and the difference matters.

A 95% confidence interval is built by a recipe with this property: if you ran the recipe on many samples, 95% of the intervals it produced would contain the true value. That is a statement about the recipe, not about the particular interval on your screen.

Your interval either contains the truth or it does not; there is no probability left in it once it is computed. What you know is that it came from a machine that gets it right nineteen times in twenty.

The figure makes this concrete by running the machine repeatedly and coloring the misses. About one in twenty comes out red, and there is nothing to distinguish a red one from a blue one except knowing the answer — which is precisely what you do not have.

Is the distinction pedantic? Sometimes. It stops being pedantic the moment you have a reason to think this sample is unusual, because the guarantee is an average over samples and says nothing about the one in front of you. Getting a probability about your interval requires a prior, which is module 10.

Check yourself

You compute a 95% confidence interval of [18.2%, 21.8%] for a volatility. Which statement is correct?

A property of the procedure. The third answer is a different error worth naming — confusing an interval for a parameter with an interval for future observations, which is much wider. And the first answer is what almost everyone means and is only strictly available in the Bayesian framing, where it requires you to have stated a prior first.

Question 1 · chapter 01

To halve a standard error, how much more data do you need?

Four times. Which makes precision expensive in a very specific way: going from a 2-point error to a 1-point error costs as much data as everything you already collected, three times over.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Five years of daily returns on a 32%-volatility stock. How precisely do you know its annual drift?

±28 points, and that is with five years. The drift is a fraction of a percent a day and the noise is 2% a day, so the noise is several hundred times bigger than the thing you are trying to see. This is the structural reason expected-return estimates are so much shakier than they look.
Question 3 · chapter 03

One year of daily returns on a 20%-volatility stock. How precisely do you know the volatility?

Under two points, from the same year that told you nothing about drift. The formula is σ/√(2n): 20% over √504 is 0.89 points of standard error, so roughly ±1.75 at 95%. That asymmetry between the two estimates is the most useful thing in this module.
Question 4 · chapter 03

How much data pins a 20% volatility to within ±1 point at 95%?

Three years for a single point. And 193 days for two points — the last point of precision costs four times what the first two did, which is √n doing its usual work.
Question 5 · chapter 04

What is the bootstrap's main weakness?

It cannot invent an unobserved crash. Which makes it excellent for the middle of a distribution — medians, ratios, drawdowns within the observed range — and unsuitable for exactly the tail questions people most want answered.
Question 6 · chapter 05

What does a 95% confidence interval guarantee?

A property of the recipe, not of your one result. The third answer describes a prediction interval, which is a different and much wider thing — the interval for where the next observation lands, rather than for where the parameter is.

Every standard error here assumes independent observations, and financial returns are not independent — volatility clusters, so effective sample sizes are smaller than the raw counts and every interval on this page is optimistically narrow. The σ/√(2n) formula for a volatility's standard error additionally assumes the returns are normal; under the fat tails of module 07 the estimator is noisier than that, and the discrepancy grows with the kurtosis. The bootstrap makes no distributional assumption but inherits the independence one, and a block bootstrap is the standard repair. And the whole module treats the true volatility as a fixed number to be estimated, which it is not — it moves, and the interesting question is usually forecasting it rather than measuring what it was.
