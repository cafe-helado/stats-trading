How many ways can this happen?

Every probability in this series is a count divided by a count. Before any of it means anything, you need to be able to count things you could never list — the orderings of a deck, the five-card hands, the ways a hundred coin flips can give sixty heads.

Scroll ↓

Start here

This is the first module and the most mechanical. A probability is favorable outcomes over total outcomes, so counting comes before everything — and the counts get large enough, fast enough, that listing stops being an option almost immediately.

  1. Chapter 02 holds the one distinction that matters. Arrangements against selections. If order counts you are multiplying; if it does not you are multiplying and then dividing by r!, and that divisor is the entire difference.
  2. Chapter 03 is the famous one. Twenty-three people and the odds of a shared birthday pass a half — 50.73%. It feels wrong because people count people when the thing doing the work is pairs, and twenty-three people make 253 of them.
  3. Chapter 05 is why this module comes first. The coefficient sitting in front of the binomial distribution is not a constant anyone chose. It is a count, and it is the count this whole page is about.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
n!Factorial — n × (n−1) × … × 1. The number of ways to arrange n distinct things. 0! is 1, which is a convention that makes everything else work.
P(n, r)Permutations: ordered selections of r things from n. Equal to n! ÷ (n−r)!.
C(n, r)Combinations: unordered selections. P(n, r) ÷ r!, written "n choose r".
nrSelections with repetition allowed — you put each one back before drawing the next.
Pascal's triangleThe combinations arranged in rows. Each entry is the sum of the two above it.
ComplementCounting the ways something does not happen and subtracting. Often far easier than the direct count.
01

Multiply the choices

One rule generates everything else on this page: if a first choice has a ways and a second has b, the pair has a × b.

Everything starts with a rule so obvious it barely reads as mathematics. Make one choice with a options and a second with b options, and there are a × b ways to make both. Extend it and every counting question on this page follows.

A four-digit PIN drawn from ten digits, repeats allowed, is 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. Forbid repeats and each choice shrinks the pool: 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5,040. That second pattern — the pool shrinking by one each time — is a permutation, written P(10, 4).

Now watch how fast this gets away from you. Arrange a full deck of 52 cards and the count is 52!, which is about 8.07 × 1067. There are roughly 1080 atoms in the observable universe. Shuffle a deck properly and you have, with near certainty, produced an ordering that has never existed before and never will again.

That is the practical point of this module. You cannot enumerate these. You have to count them without listing them, and the way to do it is to keep asking: how many choices, and how many options at each?

nr ≥ P(n, r) = n × (n−1) × … × (n−r+1) = n! ÷ (n−r)! ≥ C(n, r)

Reads as: allowing repeats gives the most, ordered selections without repeats gives fewer, and ignoring order gives fewest. Every counting problem is deciding which of the three you are in.

Check yourself

A padlock has four dials of ten digits. How many settings?

10,000. The first option is P(10, 4), which would be right if the dials somehow could not repeat a digit. Deciding whether repetition is allowed is the first question in every counting problem and the one people skip.
02

When order stops mattering

A poker hand is the same hand however it was dealt. Divide the ordered count by the number of orderings and you have the unordered one.

Most real questions do not care about order. A five-card hand is the same hand whichever sequence it arrived in; a lottery ticket does not care which number you wrote first. So the permutation count is too big — by exactly the number of ways the chosen items could have been arranged among themselves.

That number is r!, and dividing by it gives the combination:

For five cards from fifty-two, the ordered count is 311,875,200. Every hand appears in that list 5! = 120 times, once for each dealing order. So the number of distinct hands is 311,875,200 ÷ 120 = 2,598,960.

Two facts worth carrying. A 6/49 lottery has C(49, 6) = 13,983,816 tickets, which is where "one in fourteen million" comes from. And combinations are symmetric: C(52, 5) = C(52, 47), because choosing five cards to take is identical to choosing forty-seven to leave.

The symmetry is not a curiosity — it is often the shortcut. Counting what you exclude is frequently easier than counting what you include, and chapter 03 turns entirely on that idea.

C(n, r) = P(n, r) ÷ r! = n! ÷ ( r! · (n−r)! ) and C(n, r) = C(n, n−r)

Reads as: take the ordered count and divide out the orderings you do not care about. The symmetry on the right says choosing what to take and choosing what to leave are the same act.

Try this

Set n to 52 and drag r from 1 to 52. Find where the combination count peaks, and predict where before you drag.

Check yourself

Why divide the permutation count by r! rather than by something else?

Every unordered set is over-counted exactly r! times. This is worth being able to say out loud, because it is the same argument used every time something is counted with a symmetry in it — count as if the symmetry did not exist, then divide by the size of the symmetry.
03

Twenty-three people

The odds of a shared birthday in a room pass a half at twenty-three. The reason it feels wrong is that people count people.

Put people in a room one at a time and ask when two of them probably share a birthday. Almost everyone guesses something near 180. The answer is 23.

Counting it directly is a nightmare — two sharing, three sharing, two pairs sharing. Counting the complement is easy. For nobody to share, the second person must avoid one date, the third must avoid two, and so on:

At 23 people that product is 0.4927, so the chance of at least one shared birthday is 0.5073. At 22 it is 47.57% — just under. At 57 people it passes 99%, and at 70 it is 99.92%.

Now the intuition, which is the real content of the chapter. You are not asking about 23 people. You are asking about every pair of them, and 23 people make C(23, 2) = 253 pairs. Each pair matches with probability 1/365, so the expected number of matches is 253/365 = 0.693 — comfortably around one. Treating those pairs as roughly independent gives 1 − e^(−0.693) = 0.5000, against the exact 0.5073. Close enough to show that pairs are what is doing the work.

And here is the question people confuse it with: what is the chance somebody shares a birthday with you specifically? That one grows the way intuition expects. Twenty-three others gives only 6.1%, and you need 253 others before it reaches a coin flip. Which is the same 253 — one person against 253 others, or 23 people making 253 pairs. The count of pairs was always the quantity that mattered.

P(no shared birthday) = (365/365) × (364/365) × … × (365−n+1)/365

Reads as: each new person has to dodge every birthday already in the room. The dodging gets harder with every arrival, which is why the product collapses so much faster than people expect.

Where this comes from

The direct count is hopeless because "at least one shared birthday" is a union of overlapping cases. The complement is a single clean product: person 1 is free, person 2 has 364 acceptable dates out of 365, person 3 has 363, and so on down to 365 − n + 1. Multiplying and subtracting from one gives the answer exactly.

The pair argument explains why it is so fast. There are C(n, 2) = n(n−1)/2 pairs, growing with the square of the people. At n = 23 that is 253 pairs against only 23 people, and 253/365 is already about 0.69 expected matches. Anything counted over pairs grows quadratically while intuition tracks the linear count of people, and that gap is the whole illusion.

Where it breaks: the calculation assumes birthdays are uniform across 365 dates and independent between people. Neither is true — real birth dates cluster by season and by day of week, and leap years add a 366th. Both effects push in the same direction: any clustering makes matches more likely, so 23 is a mild overestimate of what a real room needs. Twins in the room break independence outright.

Check yourself

Why does 23 feel far too small?

Pairs, not people. The count that matters grows quadratically while the one people watch grows linearly. The same substitution is behind the question this gets confused with — matching you specifically needs 253 others, because then there really are only 253 pairs in play.
04

Every poker hand, counted from scratch

The rankings are not conventions. They are a frequency table, and you can derive the whole thing with chapter 02.

Poker hand rankings look arbitrary until you count them. They are ordered by exactly one thing: how rare each is out of the 2,598,960 five-card hands. Every number below comes from multiplying choices.

Four of a kind. Pick the rank, take all four suits, then any one of the 48 remaining cards: 13 × 1 × 48 = 624.

Full house. Pick a rank for the trips and three of its four suits, then a different rank for the pair and two of its suits: 13 × 4 × 12 × 6 = 3,744.

Two pair. Choose two ranks from thirteen, two suits from four in each, then a kicker from the 44 cards of other ranks: 78 × 6 × 6 × 44 = 123,552.

Flush. Any five of one suit's thirteen, across four suits, minus the forty that are also straights: 4 × 1,287 − 40 = 5,108.

Add every category and you get 2,598,960 exactly — which is the check that the counting was right, and the reason to do it this way rather than look the numbers up.

And it settles something that looks like a convention. A flush has 5,108 ways and a straight has 10,200. The flush is almost exactly twice as rare, so it outranks the straight. Nobody decided that; it fell out of the arithmetic.

Check yourself

Why does a flush beat a straight?

5,108 against 10,200. Every ranking in the game is this comparison. It is also a good check on your counting: if you derive the categories and they do not sum to 2,598,960, you have double-counted or missed something, and the sum tells you before anyone else does.
05

Where the binomial coefficient comes from

The number in front of the binomial distribution is not a constant. It is C(n, k), counting the orders in which the successes could have arrived.

Flip a fair coin ten times. The chance of any one specific sequence is (1/2)10 = 1/1024 — every sequence is equally likely. So why is five heads so much more common than ten?

Because there is only one sequence with ten heads, and there are 252 with five. The probability of a count is the probability of one sequence multiplied by how many sequences give that count, and that multiplier is exactly C(n, k).

So P(5 heads in 10) = 252/1024 = 24.61%, against P(10 heads) = 1/1024 = 0.098%. Arrange the coefficients in rows and you have Pascal's triangle, in which each entry is the sum of the two above — because a set of size k either contains the new item or does not.

Every row sums to 2n: row 10 sums to 1,024, which is every possible sequence counted once. That is the check that the binomial distribution's probabilities add to one, and it is a counting fact rather than a probabilistic one.

When module 07 writes the binomial distribution, the C(n, k) in front of it is this and nothing else — the number of ways the successes could have been arranged. Every distribution in this series that counts things has a combinatorial argument underneath it, and this is the argument.

P(exactly k successes in n trials) = C(n, k) · pk · (1−p)n−k

Reads as: the chance of one particular sequence, times how many sequences give that count. The right-hand pair is the probability of a specific run; the C(n, k) is the counting this page has been about.

Check yourself

Ten flips. Why is five heads more likely than ten heads if every sequence is equally likely?

252 sequences against one. The first answer is true but is a description rather than a reason — the reason is the count. And the third is the gambler's fallacy, which module 02 takes apart: nothing corrects anything, there are just more ways to land near the middle.

Drill it

Permutations and combinations first, then the counts that need a complement or a case split.

The first tier is mechanical — recognize which count you are in and apply it. The second is the harder skill: setting a counting problem up so that it becomes mechanical.

Question 1 · chapter 01

How many ways can a deck of 52 cards be ordered?

52! ≈ 8.07 × 10⁶⁷. The third option is C(52, 5), the number of five-card hands, which is chapter 02's number. A properly shuffled deck is, with near certainty, in an order no deck has ever been in.
Question 2 · chapter 02

What separates P(n, r) from C(n, r)?

Exactly r!. For five cards that is 120, so 311,875,200 ordered deals collapse to 2,598,960 distinct hands. The pattern generalizes: count as if the symmetry did not exist, then divide by the size of the symmetry.
Question 3 · chapter 03

How many people before a shared birthday is more likely than not?

23. The third option is where it passes 99% and the fourth is the number of pairs those 23 people make — which is what makes it work, and also the number of others you would need for a coin flip on matching you specifically.
Question 4 · chapter 03

Why is the birthday problem solved by counting the complement?

One product instead of a union. Counting what you do not want and subtracting is one of the two or three most useful moves in combinatorics, and it appears again in chapter 04 — a flush is counted as all same-suit hands minus the ones that are also straights.
Question 5 · chapter 04

There are 5,108 flushes and 10,200 straights. What does that tell you?

Rarity is the ranking. And summing every category to exactly 2,598,960 is the check that the counting was done right — if the categories do not add up, something has been double-counted.
Question 6 · chapter 05

What is the C(n, k) doing in front of the binomial distribution?

It is a count, not a constant. The normalization in the first option is a consequence rather than the purpose — the rows of Pascal's triangle sum to 2ⁿ, which is why the probabilities add to one. Everything in module 07 that counts discrete events rests on this.

The birthday calculation assumes birthdays are spread uniformly over 365 dates and are independent between people. Both are false: real births cluster by season and by weekday, and leap years supply a 366th date. Clustering makes matches more likely, so 23 slightly overstates what a real room needs, and twins break independence outright. The poker counts assume a single 52-card deck, five cards dealt at once, and no wild cards or jokers — adding a joker changes every category and, notably, makes a flush harder to rank cleanly against a full house. Factorials above about 170 overflow ordinary floating point, so this page computes large counts through log-gamma and rounds back, which is exact for the values shown here and drifts in the last digit far beyond them. And a count is only a probability when every outcome is equally likely — a shuffled deck qualifies, a room of people does not quite, and most real situations do not at all, which is what the rest of this series is about.
