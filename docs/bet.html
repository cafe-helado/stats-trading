The same arithmetic, in four different chairs

A sportsbook, a card counter, a poker player and an options market maker are running one business with four sets of vocabulary. This is that business: where the price comes from, how much to bet, why the correct answer is always smaller than the formula says, and what it takes to know you have an edge at all.

Start here

This is the capstone. Every module before it built a piece of machinery; this one points all of it at things people do with money. Nothing new is introduced — chapters 01 through 05 are odds, expected value, sampling error and tails wearing different clothes.

  1. Chapter 01 is the business model. A book quoting −110 on both sides keeps 4.55% of everything staked while forecasting nothing. An options market maker quoting 1.90 at 2.10 is running the identical trade.
  2. Chapter 02 is the sizing rule, and chapter 03 is why you should use half of it. Full Kelly halves your bankroll at some point with probability one half. Half Kelly keeps 75% of the growth and drops that risk to 12.5%.
  3. Chapter 04 is the number to carry out of the whole series. The years it takes to prove you have an edge is 4 ÷ Sharpe², in every seat, with all units canceled.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
−110American odds: risk $110 to win $100. Implies 52.38%.
OverroundHow much the implied probabilities of a market sum past 100%. The book's built-in margin.
HoldThe fraction of everything staked that a balanced book keeps. Smaller than the overround, and the number that matters.
BankrollThe money you are betting with, as opposed to the money you are betting.
f*The Kelly stake: the fraction of bankroll that maximizes long-run growth.
bb/100Poker's unit — big blinds won per hundred hands. A good live win rate is 5; the standard deviation around it is about 90.
Sharpe ratioEdge divided by the volatility around it, annualized. The one number that compares seats.
Risk of ruinThe probability of ever falling to some fraction of the starting bankroll, over an unlimited number of bets.
01

Where the price comes from

A book that cannot predict anything still makes 4.55% of everything staked. That is the whole business, and a market maker runs it too.

A standard American sports market quotes −110 on both sides: risk $110 to win $100, either way. Module 03 converted that to a probability — 52.381%. Both sides at once sum to 104.762%.

That excess is the overround: 4.762 points of probability that do not exist. It is not a mistake and it is not a forecast. It is the price of the service.

Follow the cash rather than the percentages. Two bettors each stake $110, one on each side. Whichever wins is paid $210 — their stake back plus $100. The book took in $220 and paid out $210, keeping $10. As a fraction of what was staked that is 4.545%, and it arrives with certainty. The book did not need an opinion about the game.

The mirror image is what the bettor faces. To break even at −110 you must win 52.381% of your bets — you have to be right 2.38 percentage points more often than a coin just to stand still. A bettor with no edge at all, picking at exactly 50%, loses 4.545% of every dollar they stake, forever.

Now the same trade in a different chair. An option is worth $2.00 and a market maker quotes it 1.90 bid, 2.10 offer. Someone sells to them at 1.90, someone else buys from them at 2.10, and the maker keeps $0.20 on $4.00 of two-sided business — 5.00%. Same structure, same indifference to who is right, same requirement: stay balanced. A book with a lopsided position and a market maker with a lopsided inventory have precisely the same problem.

hold = overround ÷ (1 + overround)

Reads as: the margin, spread over all the money rather than over one side of it. A 4.762-point overround is a 4.545% hold, and the hold is the number that describes the business.

Try this

Find the price at which the book keeps a tenth of everything staked, then find the one where it keeps nothing. Predict the second before you drag.

Check yourself

A book takes $110 on each side of a −110 market. What is its expected profit?

$10, and not as an expectation. A balanced book has no exposure to the outcome — its profit is realized whatever happens. This is why the actual job of a bookmaker, like the actual job of a market maker, is moving prices to attract the side they are short of. Forecasting is a means to balancing, not the product.
02

How much to bet

Maximize the average of the logarithm rather than the average of the money, and one specific fraction falls out.

Suppose you find the other side of chapter 01 — a bet where you genuinely have the edge. How much of your bankroll should go on it?

Maximizing expected money gives a useless answer: bet everything, every time, because that maximizes the average. Module 05 showed why the average is the wrong target for anything repeated — you go broke on the first loss, and the average is carried by paths you will never be on.

Maximize the expected logarithm of the bankroll instead, which is the same as maximizing the median long-run outcome, and the answer is the Kelly criterion:

For a coin landing heads 60% of the time at even money, that gives f* = 0.20 — bet a fifth of the bankroll. The growth rate at that stake is 2.03% per bet. Bet 10% instead and you grow at 1.50%; bet 30% and you grow at 1.47%. The peak is flat near the top, which turns out to matter enormously in chapter 03.

Two properties are worth internalizing. First, the growth curve comes back down and crosses zero at about twice the optimal stake — in the continuous case, exactly twice. Betting double Kelly gives you all of the volatility and none of the growth, which is a remarkably precise description of a lot of trading. Second, a bigger edge does not mean a bigger bet: a +400 longshot you think wins 25% of the time carries a 25% edge but a Kelly stake of only 6.25%, while a −200 favorite with an 8% edge takes 16%. The stake follows edge divided by variance, and the longshot has far more of the latter.

f* = (b·p − q) / b and in continuous form f* = μ / σ²

Reads as: stake your edge divided by the odds, or in the continuous version, your edge divided by your variance. Both say the same thing — the size of a bet is set by how much you expect to make relative to how much the answer bounces around.

Try this

Set the win probability to exactly the break-even rate and watch what the optimal stake does. Then push it one tenth of a point above.

Check yourself

Bet A has a 25% edge; bet B has an 8% edge. Which takes the bigger Kelly stake?

Edge alone does not size a bet. This is the same statement as module 11's: a number is meaningless until you know the spread around it. A large edge on something wild is a small bet; a modest edge on something tame is a large one.
03

Why nobody bets that much

Full Kelly halves your bankroll at some point with probability one half. Half Kelly costs a quarter of the growth and removes almost all of that.

Kelly is optimal for a stated objective — maximum long-run growth — and that objective quietly assumes you will still be there for the long run. The path is brutal.

There is a clean result for how brutal. Betting at a k-multiple of full Kelly, the probability of ever seeing your bankroll fall to a fraction a of where it started is a(2/k − 1). At full Kelly the exponent is 1, so the probability of ever halving is exactly 50%, of ever quartering exactly 25%, of ever falling by 90% exactly 10%. Those are not tail scenarios. They are coin flips.

Now the other half of the trade. Growth at a k-multiple is 2k − k² of the maximum. At half Kelly you keep 75% of the growth for half the volatility, and the risk of ever halving falls from 50% to 12.5%. At quarter Kelly you keep 43.8% and the risk drops to 0.78%.

That asymmetry is the whole argument, and it exists because the growth curve is flat near its peak while the risk is not. Nobody serious bets full Kelly. Half is the common answer and a quarter is defensible, and the reason is not timidity — it is that the formula's input is an estimate, which is chapter 05.

Where this comes from

Under the continuous approximation a bankroll bet at fraction f of a strategy with drift μ and volatility σ grows at g(f) = fμ − f²σ²/2. Setting the derivative to zero gives f* = μ/σ², and substituting back gives a maximum growth of μ²/2σ², which is half the squared Sharpe ratio. Writing f = k·f* and substituting gives g = (2k − k²)·gmax directly. The drawdown result comes from the same diffusion: the probability of a geometric Brownian motion with drift ever reaching a level below its start is the ratio raised to a power set by drift over variance, which for a k-multiple of Kelly works out to 2/k − 1.

Where it breaks: all of this is the continuous limit. For a discrete bet the growth curve crosses zero slightly before twice the Kelly stake — at 1.99× for a 55% coin, 1.95× at 60%, and 1.79× at 70%, because a discrete bet cannot be rebalanced mid-move. The bigger the individual bet, the further the approximation drifts, and in the direction that flatters you. It also assumes you can always stake a fraction rather than a minimum ticket, which is exactly false at a blackjack table.

Check yourself

Why is half Kelly the common answer rather than a compromise nobody can justify?

The two curves have different shapes. Growth is quadratic near its maximum, so it barely moves when you back off; the probability of ruin is a power law in the same parameter, so it collapses. Any time you find an optimum that is flat on one axis and steep on another, sitting below the peak is nearly free.
04

Four seats, one arithmetic

Sports betting, card counting, poker and selling volatility, with the vocabulary stripped out. The units cancel and one number is left.

Put four apparently unrelated ways of making money side by side, each described by only three things: the edge per unit staked, the standard deviation per unit, and how many units happen in a year.

Sports betting. Winning 55% of −110 sides is a 4.5% edge per bet with a standard deviation of about 1.0, over maybe 500 bets a year. Annual Sharpe 1.01.

Card counting. A 1% average edge — smaller than any other seat here — with a per-hand standard deviation of 1.15, but 20,000 hands a year. Annual Sharpe 1.23, the second highest in the table, from the smallest edge in it.

Live poker. Five big blinds per hundred hands against a standard deviation of ninety, over 30,000 hands a year. Annual Sharpe 0.96 — the largest per-unit edge and the worst ratio, because live poker deals so few hands.

Selling volatility. 15% of alpha a year against 10% of volatility, marked daily. Annual Sharpe 1.50.

And now the number that makes the whole series cohere. Module 08 showed that a mean needs (2/SR)² observations before it is two standard errors from zero. Convert that to years and the units cancel completely:

A Sharpe of 1.0 takes four years. A Sharpe of 1.5 takes 1.78. A Sharpe of 0.5 takes sixteen. This is true for a card counter, a poker player, a sports bettor and a hedge fund identically, and it does not care how many bets a year you make — a strategy trading a thousand times a day and one trading once a month need exactly the same number of years to prove the same Sharpe ratio.

years to t = 2 = 4 ÷ (annual Sharpe)²

Reads as: how long before your results distinguish you from someone with no edge at all. Nothing about the size of the bets, the frequency, or the market survives into this formula.

Try this

Take the card counter's edge down from 1% to 0.5% and watch the years double twice over. Then put the frequency up and watch it come back.

Check yourself

A counter has a 1% edge per hand and a poker player has a 5% edge per hundred hands. Who has the better business?

Frequency is what makes an edge bankable. The units differing is precisely what the Sharpe ratio is for — it divides by the standard deviation and multiplies by the square root of the count, and everything idiosyncratic cancels. This is also the honest reason high-frequency trading exists: a per-trade edge far too small to notice becomes a Sharpe nobody else can reach, purely from the count.
05

Where it breaks

Kelly tells the vol seller to run fifteen times leverage. Chapter 04 says it takes 1.78 years to establish the number that produced it, and module 12 says the returns are not the shape assumed.

Run the Kelly formula on the fourth seat exactly as written. Edge 15%, volatility 10%, so f* = μ/σ² = 15× leverage. Every part of that calculation is correct and the answer is insane. It is worth being precise about why, because all three reasons are things this series has already shown.

The inputs are estimates, and bad ones. The 1.5 Sharpe that produced 15× is exactly the quantity chapter 04 says takes 1.78 years of data to distinguish from zero. Sizing off a number with that much sampling error is not aggressive; it is arithmetic performed on noise. Kelly is also asymmetric here: overestimating your edge by a factor of two puts you at double Kelly, where growth is zero, and by a factor of three puts you well past it, where growth is negative.

The distribution is wrong. The continuous derivation assumes a Gaussian. Module 12 showed a mild two-regime mixture whose 99% expected shortfall is 63% worse than a normal's, and short-volatility returns are considerably more skewed than that mixture. A Kelly stake computed from μ and σ alone is blind to precisely the shape that ends leveraged positions.

Ruin is not a fraction. The formula assumes you can always stake a proportion of what remains. A margin call, a risk limit, a table minimum and a redemption are all the same event: a floor that turns a drawdown into an exit, at which point the long run the whole derivation is about no longer applies to you.

None of this makes the framework useless — it makes it a ceiling rather than a target. The correct reading of Kelly is: this is the most that could ever be justified, under assumptions you know are optimistic, on inputs you cannot measure well. Take a fraction of it. And notice that the argument arrives at the same place as module 12's from the opposite direction: be precise about the middle, conservative about the tail, and size so that being wrong is survivable.

Check yourself

You size at full Kelly on an edge you have overestimated by a factor of two. What happens?

Double the true Kelly stake, which is the zero-growth point. This is the strongest practical argument for fractional Kelly, and it is not about risk appetite: if you size at half Kelly and your edge estimate is double the truth, you land on full Kelly of the real edge — still the growth-maximizing bet. The fraction buys you a margin of error in the input, not just a smoother ride.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Both sides of a market are priced at −110. What does a balanced book keep?

4.545%, and with certainty. Two $110 stakes bring in $220 and pay out $210. The overround and the hold are different quantities and the second is the one that describes the business — hold = overround ÷ (1 + overround).
Question 2 · chapter 01

What win rate does a bettor need at −110 just to break even?

52.381%, which is the price itself read as a probability. A bettor picking at a true 50% loses 4.545% of every dollar staked — the same number the book keeps, seen from the other chair.
Question 3 · chapter 02

A coin lands heads 60% of the time, paying even money. What is the Kelly stake?

20%, growing the bankroll 2.03% per bet. "Bet everything" is what maximizing expected money actually recommends, and it goes broke with probability one — which is the whole reason the objective is the logarithm instead.
Question 4 · chapter 03

Betting full Kelly, what is the probability of ever seeing your bankroll halve?

A coin flip. The probability of ever falling to a fraction a is a(2/k − 1), so at full Kelly it is just a. Half Kelly keeps 75% of the growth while cutting that to 12.5%, and the asymmetry exists because growth is flat near its peak while risk of ruin is not.
Question 5 · chapter 04

How long does it take to establish a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 at two standard errors?

Four years, and the trade frequency cancels out entirely. A strategy trading a thousand times a day and one trading monthly need the same number of years to prove the same Sharpe. Frequency raises the Sharpe you can achieve; it does not shorten the time to prove the one you have.
Question 6 · chapter 05

Kelly says a Sharpe-1.5 strategy at 10% volatility should run 15× leverage. What is wrong with doing that?

Correct arithmetic on inputs you cannot measure. Overestimating your edge by a factor of two puts you at double Kelly, where growth is zero. Sizing at half Kelly means that same error lands you on the true full Kelly instead — the fraction buys a margin of error in the input, not just a calmer ride.

The four seats are parameterized from figures that are conventional rather than measured, and each hides something. The card-counting edge assumes bet spreading, which raises both the edge and the variance above the flat-bet figures used here, and it ignores that a counter only plays a fraction of the hands dealt. Live poker win rates above five big blinds per hundred are rare and self-reported ones are selected on having worked. The sports figure assumes a 55% win rate persists, which is a much stronger claim than it looks — the market moves against a bettor who has one. And the volatility seat's 15% alpha at 10% volatility is a smooth description of a return stream whose defining property is that it is not smooth. Every Kelly figure on this page assumes a known edge and a Gaussian, and chapter 05 is about how much both assumptions are worth. Nothing here is advice about money; it is the arithmetic that any advice would have to be consistent with.
