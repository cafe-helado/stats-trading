Five shapes, and what each is for

The normal is not the only distribution and usually not the right one. Here are the four you will actually meet, what question each one answers, and the single diagnostic that tells them apart from a sample.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Module 03 showed that sums of independent things go normal. This one is about everything that is not a sum of independent things — prices, which multiply; counts, which are integers; and returns, which have tails a normal cannot produce.

  1. Chapter 01 is the one finance is built on. Prices multiply rather than add, so the normal belongs on the log return and the lognormal on the price. Which is why halving is more likely than doubling.
  2. Chapter 04 is the surprise. A mixture of two calm-and-stressed regimes produces tails 116 times fatter than a normal four standard deviations out — and thinner tails two standard deviations out. Fat tails come with a thin shoulder.
  3. Chapter 05 is the practical skill: given a sample, which of these is it? One number does most of the work.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
LognormalA quantity whose logarithm is normal. What you get when many small multiplicative shocks compound.
Log returnln(price now ÷ price before). Adds across periods, where percentage returns do not.
PoissonThe distribution of how many rare independent events happen in a fixed window. Its variance equals its mean.
λThe Poisson's one parameter: the average number of events per window.
Student's tA bell with fatter tails, controlled by its degrees of freedom. As df → ∞ it becomes the normal.
Degrees of freedomThe t's tail-fatness dial. Low means fat; its excess kurtosis is 6/(df − 4).

Excess kurtosisZero for a normal. The single most useful number for telling these shapes apart from data.
01

Prices multiply, so the normal goes on the logarithm

A stock cannot go negative and a 10% gain does not undo a 10% loss. Both facts point at the same distribution.

Start with the arithmetic everyone has met and nobody quite believes. Up 10%, then down 10%, leaves you at 0.99 — not back where you started. Percentage returns do not add.

Their logarithms do. ln(1.10) = 0.0953 and ln(0.90) = −0.1054; they sum to −0.0101, and exponentiating gives 0.9900 exactly. So if you want a quantity that behaves like a sum — and module 03 says sums go normal — you want the log return, not the percentage one.

Make the log return normal and the price becomes lognormal, which fixes the other problem too: a lognormal cannot go below zero, and a stock cannot either. Model returns as plain normal and you assign real probability to a negative price — 0.043% at 30% annual volatility, which is small and is still nonsense.

The consequence people find hardest is the asymmetry. At 30% volatility and no drift, the chance a stock halves over a year is 1.54% while the chance it doubles is 0.69% — less than half as likely, despite both being "a factor of two." The mean sits above the median, and most outcomes land below the mean: 55.96% of them.

price is lognormal ⟺ ln(price) is normal ⟺ returns compound

Reads as: the three statements are the same statement. Choosing a lognormal for the price is not a modeling flourish — it is what you get automatically once you accept that returns multiply.

Check yourself

A stock at 30% volatility, no drift. Is it more likely to halve or to double over a year?

Halving, by more than two to one. The two moves are symmetric in log space but the log return is centered at −σ²/2 rather than at zero, which is what makes them asymmetric in price. That −σ²/2 is not a fudge: it is what forces the mean price to come out right when the median is what the normal is centered on.
02

Counting rare things

The Poisson has one parameter and one signature: its variance equals its mean. If a count does not obey that, it is not Poisson.

Some quantities are not measurements, they are counts: how many trading halts this year, how many defaults in a portfolio, how many customers arrive in an hour, how many of your short options get assigned. Counts are integers, cannot be negative, and have no natural upper bound.

The distribution for that is the Poisson, and it has exactly one parameter — λ, the average count per window. Everything else follows. If an exchange sees an average of three halts a year, the chance of a year with none is 4.98% and the chance of six or more is 8.39%.

Its diagnostic is unusually clean: the variance equals the mean. A Poisson with λ = 4 has a standard deviation of exactly 2. If you have count data whose variance is much larger than its mean, it is not Poisson — the events are clustering, which is a real and important finding rather than a modeling inconvenience.

P(k events) = e−λ λᵏ / k! mean = λ, variance = λ

Reads as: one parameter sets everything, and the spread is locked to the average. That lock is the test — real count data that breaks it is telling you the events are not independent.

Try this

Set λ to 1 and predict the most likely number of events. There are two answers and that is not a mistake.

Check yourself

You count defaults per year over a long history: mean 4, variance 19. What does that tell you?

Clustering, and it is the most important thing in the data. Independent arrivals give variance equal to mean; correlated ones give more. In credit that correlation is the entire risk — a portfolio of a hundred loans that default independently is safe, and the same hundred loans defaulting together is not, and the mean default rate is identical in both worlds.
03

Student's t, and why it is the honest default

A bell with a dial for tail fatness. Turn it to five and you have something that fits real returns.

The normal's problem is not its middle, it is its edges. Three standard deviations out it puts 0.27% of the mass, and real financial returns put far more there.

Student's t is the same bell shape with a dial: its degrees of freedom control how fat the tails are, and as the dial goes to infinity it becomes exactly the normal. At 5 degrees of freedom the mass beyond three sigma is 3.01% — 11 times the normal's. At 10 df it is 1.33%, about five times. At 30 df it is twice.

The dial has a direct reading in excess kurtosis: 6/(df − 4). So t with 5 df has excess kurtosis 6.0, t with 6 df has 3.0, and t with 10 df has 1.0. Daily equity returns typically measure somewhere between 3 and 8, which is why 5 or 6 degrees of freedom is the usual fit and why anyone who models daily returns as normal is choosing a distribution they know is wrong in the one place it matters.

Try this

Set the degrees of freedom to 30 and see how little is left of the difference. Then drop it to 4 and watch the ratio panel.

Check yourself

Why does the t's excess kurtosis formula, 6/(df − 4), stop working at four degrees of freedom?

The moment stops existing. A t with 4 or fewer degrees of freedom has infinite kurtosis, and one with 2 or fewer has infinite variance — at which point the central limit theorem from module 03 stops applying. That is not a curiosity: it is the boundary between "fat tails I can model" and "a quantity whose average is not a meaningful summary."
04

The cheapest fat tail, and its thin shoulder

Mix two normals and you get tails 116 times fatter than a normal — and, four steps earlier, tails that are thinner.

Here is the least exotic way to build a fat tail. Suppose the market is calm 90% of the time, at 15% volatility, and stressed the other 10%, at 45%. Each regime is perfectly normal. The mixture is not.

Its overall volatility is 20.12% and its excess kurtosis is 5.33 — squarely in the range real equity returns show, built out of nothing but two normals and a coin. This is why regime-switching is the default explanation for fat tails: you do not need an exotic distribution, only the observation that markets have quiet periods and loud ones.

Now the part that surprises people. Compare the mixture to a single normal with the same overall volatility:

  • Beyond 2σ: the mixture has 4.37% against the normal's 4.55% — thinner.
  • Beyond 3σ: 1.80% against 0.27% — 6.7× fatter.
  • Beyond 4σ: 0.736% against 0.0063% — 116× fatter.
  • Beyond 5σ: 0.253% against 0.00006% — 4,414× fatter.

A fat-tailed distribution is not uniformly fatter. It has to take the mass from somewhere, and it takes it from the shoulders. Which means a model that looks conservative at two standard deviations can be wrong by two orders of magnitude at four, and testing it only in the ordinary range will never reveal that.

Try this

Make the stressed regime rarer but more violent — 2% of the time at 90% volatility — and predict what happens to the crossover point.

Check yourself

A risk model is validated against two years of daily data and matches the observed 2σ exceedances almost exactly. What does that establish?

It validates the part of the distribution nobody is worried about. Two years of daily data holds about 500 observations, roughly 23 of which should be 2σ events and none of which should be 4σ. A test with no 4σ observations in it cannot distinguish a model that is right there from one that is wrong by two orders of magnitude — which is the structural reason tail models fail their first real test rather than their validation.
05

Which one is this?

Given a sample and no label, three questions get you to the right family almost every time.

The practical version of this module is identification. You have a column of numbers; which of these shapes is it?

One — is it a count? Non-negative integers with no upper bound point at Poisson. Then check the signature: variance against mean. Equal means Poisson; much larger means clustered arrivals and a different model.

Two — is it positive and multiplicative? Prices, volumes, incomes and durations cannot go negative and tend to have a long right tail. Take logs and look again; if the logs are symmetric you have a lognormal and should be working in log space from here on.

Three — what is the excess kurtosis? This is the single most useful number. Zero says normal. Two to eight says Student's t or a mixture, with the t's degrees of freedom recoverable as 4 + 6/kurtosis. Very large, or unstable as you add data, says you may be near the boundary where the moment does not exist.

The last diagnostic deserves a warning. Kurtosis is a fourth moment, so it is enormously sensitive to the largest few observations — a single extreme day can move it substantially, and it is less stable exactly when the data is fat-tailed. Use it to choose a family, never to claim precision.

Check yourself

A sample has excess kurtosis 2.0 in the first half of the data and 9.0 in the second half. What is the most likely explanation?

A handful of points, almost certainly. The first answer might also be true — regimes are real — but it is not the parsimonious reading of a fourth moment doubling. The practical rule is to compute kurtosis, use it to pick a family, and never quote it to more than one significant figure.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Why is the normal put on log returns rather than on percentage returns?

Because compounding is multiplication, and logs turn multiplication into addition. Up 10% then down 10% is 0.99, not 1.00 — but ln(1.1) + ln(0.9) = −0.0101 exactly, and exponentiating recovers 0.99. Everything else about the lognormal follows from that one move.
Question 2 · chapter 01

At 30% volatility and no drift, which is more likely over a year?

Halving, by more than two to one. Symmetric in logs, asymmetric in price, because the log return is centered at −σ²/2. This is also why the median outcome of a volatile asset is below its mean — and why the average of many volatile assets flatters the typical one.
Question 3 · chapter 02

What is the Poisson's identifying signature?

Variance equals mean, and it is a testable claim. Count data whose variance far exceeds its mean is telling you the events cluster — which in defaults, outages or claims is usually the single most important thing in the dataset.
Question 4 · chapter 03

Beyond three standard deviations, how much more mass does a t with 5 degrees of freedom hold than a normal?

Eleven times, and the difference is entirely in the tails. The two densities are nearly identical within one standard deviation, which is exactly why fitting a model on ordinary days and trusting it on extreme ones goes wrong.
Question 5 · chapter 04

A mixture of 90% at 15 vol and 10% at 45 vol has more mass beyond 4σ than a matched normal. How much more?

Two orders of magnitude, from nothing more exotic than two normals and a coin. And at 2σ the same mixture is thinner than the normal, 4.37% against 4.55%. Fat tails are paid for out of the shoulders.
Question 6 · chapter 05

A sample has excess kurtosis of about 6. What family should you reach for?

t with 5 df, or a mixture, and the two are hard to tell apart from data. They are different stories about the same shape — one says the tails are intrinsic, the other says the world switches between calm and stressed. Which story you believe changes what you expect next, even though both fit the histogram.

Every figure here is a clean parametric distribution, which real data never is. The lognormal assumes returns are independent across periods; they are not, because volatility clusters, and that dependence fattens the tails of multi-day returns beyond anything on this page. The Poisson assumes events arrive independently at a constant rate, which almost no interesting count obeys — halts, defaults and outages all cluster, and the honest diagnostic is the variance-to-mean ratio rather than the fit. Student's t and the two-regime mixture produce very similar histograms from very different stories, and a sample of a few thousand days cannot reliably tell them apart; choosing between them is a modeling judgment, not a measurement. And every kurtosis quoted here is a fourth moment computed from a finite sample, which means it is dominated by its largest few observations and should never be trusted to more than one significant figure.
