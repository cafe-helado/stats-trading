One number for a whole cloud

Correlation compresses a scatter plot into a single figure, and everything it throws away is still there. Here is what it buys you in a portfolio, what it is not, how much it explains, how noisy it is, and four datasets with identical statistics that look nothing alike.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Everything so far has been about one quantity at a time. This module is about two, which is where portfolios, hedges and regressions live — and where a single summary number does the most damage.

  1. Chapter 01 is the practical payoff. Two equally volatile assets at zero correlation halve the variance and cut the volatility by 29.3%. At correlation one they cut nothing at all, and the curve between those is not a straight line.
  2. Chapter 03 is the corrective. A correlation of 0.7 — which anyone would call strong — leaves 51% of the variance unexplained.
  3. Chapter 05 is the warning. Four datasets with the same means, the same variances, the same correlation and the same fitted line, and four completely different pictures.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
CovarianceThe average product of two variables' deviations from their means. Has units, so it is hard to interpret directly.
ρ, rCorrelation: covariance divided by both standard deviations. Unitless, between −1 and 1, symmetric in its two arguments.
βThe slope of a regression. Unlike correlation it is not symmetric — regressing Y on X gives a different number from X on Y.
R²The share of one variable's variance explained by the other. For a simple regression it is exactly ρ².
ResidualWhat the line does not explain. Its standard deviation is σ·√(1−ρ²).
Fisher zThe transform that makes a correlation's sampling distribution roughly normal, so an interval can be built for it.
Diversification benefitHow much portfolio volatility falls below the weighted average of its parts. Zero at correlation one.
Anscombe's quartetFour datasets constructed to share every common summary statistic while looking entirely different.
01

What diversification actually buys

Two assets, same volatility, split evenly. The correlation between them decides everything and the relationship is not linear.

Take two assets, each at 20% volatility, and hold half of each. What is the portfolio's volatility?

At correlation 1 the answer is 20% — you have bought the same thing twice and gained nothing. At correlation 0 it is 14.14%, a 29.3% reduction, which is the famous 1/√2. At correlation 0.6 it is 17.89% and the benefit has already shrunk to 10.6%.

The shape of that curve is the important part. Most of the benefit is gone by the time correlation reaches 0.6, and correlations among risky assets are usually higher than that — and higher still in exactly the weeks you need the diversification. That is the finding the options series reaches from the other direction: correlation is bounded at one, and it gets there on the day you cannot afford it.

σ²_p = w²σ₁² + (1−w)²σ₂² + 2w(1−w)ρσ₁σ₂

Reads as: variances add, plus a cross term set by the correlation. Everything diversification does is in that third term, and it vanishes at ρ = 0.

Try this

Set the correlation to −1 and predict the portfolio volatility. Then find the weight that makes it zero.

Check yourself

Two assets at 20% volatility with a correlation of 0.9. How much does a 50/50 split reduce volatility?

Two and a half percent, which is nothing. Diversification is not a linear function of correlation, and at the levels typical of risky assets it delivers much less than the word suggests. A portfolio of forty highly correlated names is close to one position with a smaller ticket.
02

Correlation is symmetric; slope is not

Regress Y on X and get 2.0. Regress X on Y and get 0.125. Same data, same correlation, and the two are not reciprocals.

Take X at 10% volatility and Y at 40%, correlated 0.5. The regression slope of Y on X is ρ·σ_Y/σ_X = 2.0. The slope of X on Y is ρ·σ_X/σ_Y = 0.125.

If a slope were simply "the relationship," those two would be reciprocals — 2.0 and 0.5. They are not, and the gap is exactly the thing regression does: it minimizes error in one direction, and swapping which variable is on which axis changes the problem.

Their product is 0.25, which is ρ². That identity is worth carrying: the two slopes multiply to R², so the more they disagree, the less the relationship explains.

The practical version is hedging. If you hedge Y with X you use β = 2.0 units of X per unit of Y. If you hedge X with Y you use 0.125. Reaching for the reciprocal of the first — 0.5 — would leave you four times overhedged, and it is a mistake people make under time pressure.

βY|X = ρ · σY/σX βX|Y = ρ · σX/σY βY|X · βX|Y = ρ²

Reads as: a slope is a correlation scaled by the ratio of the two spreads. Correlation does not know which variable you care about; a slope does.

Check yourself

Stock Y has twice the volatility of index X, and they are 0.6 correlated. What is Y's beta to X?

1.2. Beta and correlation get used interchangeably and they are only equal when the two volatilities happen to match. A high-volatility stock with a modest correlation can have a large beta, and a low-volatility one with a high correlation can have a small one — which is why hedge ratios are computed rather than eyeballed.
03

How little a strong correlation explains

0.7 sounds like a lot. It accounts for 49% of the variance and leaves 51% on the table.

Correlation is on a scale that flatters it. Square it and you get R², the share of variance one variable accounts for, and the squaring is brutal.

  • ρ = 0.3 explains 9% — the residual is 95.4% as volatile as the original.
  • ρ = 0.5 explains 25% — residual 86.6%.
  • ρ = 0.7 explains 49% — residual 71.4%.
  • ρ = 0.9 explains 81% — residual 43.6%.

Read the last column rather than the first. Hedging an asset with something 0.7 correlated removes barely a quarter of the volatility, because volatility falls with √(1−ρ²) and not with ρ. A hedge that "captures 70% of the move" leaves you with 71% of the risk.

This is the single most common way correlation misleads. It is reported on a scale where 0.7 reads as most of the way to one, and it operates on a scale where 0.7 is barely past halfway.

Check yourself

You hedge a position with an instrument correlated 0.8 to it. How much of the volatility is left?

Sixty percent survives, even with a perfect hedge ratio. The third answer is 1−ρ², which is the variance left rather than the volatility — a factor-of-almost-two difference that matters because risk limits are usually set in volatility. And no hedge ratio can do better: 0.6 is the minimum, achieved exactly at β.
04

And it is noisier than you think

Thirty observations and a measured correlation of 0.3. The 95% interval includes zero.

Correlations get quoted to two decimal places from samples that cannot support one. Estimate ρ from n paired observations and the standard error is roughly (1−ρ²)/√n.

At r = 0.3 with 30 observations the standard error is 0.166, and a proper interval — using the Fisher transform, which handles the boundary at ±1 — runs from −0.068 to 0.596. It includes zero. You cannot even conclude that the two things are related.

With 250 observations the same 0.3 gets an interval of 0.187 to 0.413, which is usable. To call a correlation of 0.30 distinguishable from zero at all takes about 44 paired observations, and to pin it to ±0.05 takes several hundred.

Which sets a rule for reading correlation matrices: a matrix built from a year of monthly data has twelve observations per pair, and every number in it is noise dressed as precision.

Check yourself

A correlation matrix is built from one year of monthly returns. How much should you trust its entries?

Twelve observations is nothing. The third answer has a grain of truth — large correlations do have smaller standard errors, since the (1−ρ²) shrinks — but at n = 12 even a measured 0.7 has an interval running from about 0.2 to 0.9. A correlation matrix is a place where enormous numbers of weakly estimated quantities get treated as data.
05

Four datasets, identical statistics

Same means, same variances, same correlation, same fitted line. Now look at them.

In 1973 Frank Anscombe built four small datasets to make a point that no amount of argument had managed. Each has a mean x of 9.00, a mean y of 7.50, an x-variance of 11.00, a y-variance of 4.13, a correlation of 0.816 and a fitted slope of 0.500.

Every summary statistic anyone would compute is the same. The pictures are: a genuine linear relationship, a perfect parabola, a perfect line with one outlier, and a vertical stack of points with a single distant one that creates the entire correlation by itself.

The fourth is the one to sit with. Ten points share an identical x value, so they contain no information about slope at all. One eleventh point, far away, determines the entire fit. Remove it and the correlation is undefined; keep it and the statistics look unremarkable.

The lesson is not "always plot your data," though you should. It is that summary statistics are lossy in ways they do not advertise, and the way to find out what they dropped is to look at the thing itself.

Check yourself

In Anscombe's fourth dataset, ten of the eleven points share the same x value. What does that do?

One point is doing all the work. The correlation is well-defined and it is entirely an artifact of a single observation — remove it and there is no x-variation left to correlate with anything. This is not an exotic pathology: any regression with concentrated x values and one distant lever has the same structure, and it shows up constantly in factor models with a few extreme names.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Two assets at 20% volatility, correlation zero, split 50/50. Portfolio volatility?

14.14%. Halving the variance, not the volatility, which is the whole reason diversification underwhelms people who expect the second. And at correlation 0.9 the same split gives 19.49% — a 2.5% reduction.
Question 2 · chapter 02

X is at 10% volatility, Y at 40%, correlation 0.5. What is the beta of Y on X?

2.0, and 0.125 is the other regression. Their product is 0.25 = ρ², which is a good check: if two betas multiply to something other than R², one of them is wrong.
Question 3 · chapter 03

A correlation of 0.7. How much of the variance does it explain?

49%, and the residual volatility is 71.4% of the original. Correlation is reported on a scale that makes 0.7 look like most of the way to one, and it operates on a scale where 0.7 is barely half.
Question 4 · chapter 03

You hedge with an instrument correlated 0.6. What fraction of the volatility survives?

Eighty percent survives. A 0.6-correlated hedge removes a fifth of the risk, which is often not worth the cost and the basis exposure it introduces. The third answer is the variance rather than the volatility.
Question 5 · chapter 04

You measure a correlation of 0.30 from 30 paired observations. What can you conclude?

The interval includes zero. It takes about 44 paired observations even to distinguish a 0.30 correlation from no relationship at all — which is a useful benchmark when someone shows you a correlation matrix built from a year of monthly data.
Question 6 · chapter 05

What does Anscombe's quartet demonstrate?

Lossiness, not error. Every statistic in the quartet is correctly computed; they simply cannot distinguish a linear relationship from a parabola, an outlier, or a single leverage point. The remedy is to look at the data, and the reason it keeps needing repeating is that the statistics give no hint that anything was lost.

Correlation as used here measures linear association only: two variables can be perfectly dependent and read zero correlation, which Anscombe's second dataset nearly demonstrates and a symmetric parabola demonstrates exactly. Everything in chapters 01 and 03 assumes the correlation is stable, and financial correlations are not — they rise in stress, which is when the diversification is being counted on, and the assets in a portfolio built for its correlation matrix will not have that matrix in the week it matters. The standard errors in chapter 04 assume independent paired observations drawn from a bivariate normal; overlapping windows, autocorrelation and fat tails all make them optimistic. And none of this touches causation, which no correlation of any size and no sample of any length can establish on its own.
