An interactive derivation · No. 09

Sixty heads in a hundred. Is the coin bent?

The machinery for answering that is the most used and most misunderstood apparatus in statistics. This is what a p-value is, what it is not, the error nobody reports, and what happens to the whole edifice the twentieth time you run it.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Module 08 asked how precisely a sample pins a number down. This one asks a narrower and more common question: is what I am looking at distinguishable from nothing at all?

  1. Chapter 01 is the definition to get right. A p-value is the probability of data this extreme if the null were true. It is not the probability the null is true, and the gap between those is module 10's whole subject.
  2. Chapter 03 is the one that changes how you read results. A test of a hundred flips finds a genuinely 55/45 coin only 13.5% of the time. Most "no significant difference" findings are not evidence of no difference; they are evidence of a small sample.
  3. Chapter 06 is the one that matters on a desk. Run twenty independent tests at the 5% level with nothing real in any of them and the chance at least one comes back significant is 64%.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
H₀The null hypothesis. The dull explanation you are trying to rule out — the coin is fair, the strategy has no edge.
H₁The alternative. What you would believe instead.
p-valueP(data at least this extreme | H₀). A statement about the data given the null, never about the null given the data.
αThe significance level — how often you are willing to reject a true null. A decision made before looking.
βThe chance of missing a real effect. Power is 1 − β.
Type I / Type IICrying wolf, and missing the wolf.
Effect sizeHow big the thing you are looking for actually is. Nothing works without it.
χ²The statistic for counts in categories: summed squared gaps between observed and expected, each scaled by expected.
Family-wise errorThe chance of at least one false positive across a whole batch of tests.
01

Build a straw man, then try to knock it down

You cannot prove the coin is bent. You can only show that a fair one would rarely do what you just watched it do.

A hundred flips gave sixty heads. The question is not "is the coin bent" — the machinery cannot answer that. The question it can answer is: if the coin were perfectly fair, how often would it hand me something at least this lopsided?

That is the whole idea. Assume the dull explanation — the null hypothesis, written H₀ — then compute how surprising your data would be under it. That probability is the p-value.

For sixty heads in a hundred, counting exactly: a fair coin gives 60 or more heads 2.84% of the time. Bent could mean bent either way, so the two-sided answer doubles it: 5.69%.

Now watch something instructive. Use the normal approximation instead — z = (60 − 50)/5 = 2.00, which gives a two-sided p of 4.55%. The exact calculation says 0.0569 and the approximation says 0.0455. Against the conventional threshold of 0.05 these are opposite verdicts, on identical data. The threshold is a convention, not a discovery, and one wrong side of it is worth exactly nothing.

And the thing to carry out of this chapter above all: a p-value of 0.0569 does not mean there is a 5.69% chance the coin is fair. It means a fair coin produces results this lopsided 5.69% of the time. To get from there to a belief about the coin you need a prior, which is module 10. With an even prior between fair and 60/40-biased, sixty heads takes you to 88% on bent; with a 2% prior it takes you to 13%. Same data, same p-value, wildly different conclusions.

p = P( data at least this extreme | H₀ ) ≠ P( H₀ | data )

Reads as: the chance of the evidence given the theory, which is not the chance of the theory given the evidence. Those two are related only through a prior, and swapping them is the same mistake as reading a 99%-accurate medical test as a 99% chance of being ill.

Find the number of heads where the exact and approximate p-values fall on opposite sides of 0.05. Then push the sample to a thousand and watch the two converge.

Check yourself

A test returns p = 0.03. What does that mean?

It is a statement about the data, conditional on the null. The first and third options swap the conditional — the error module 10 exists to fix. The fourth confuses significance with size: with enough data a trivially small effect returns a tiny p-value, which is why an effect size always has to be reported next to one.
02

Two ways to be wrong, and you must pick which you fear

Crying wolf, and missing the wolf. Every threshold trades one against the other, and there is no setting that avoids both.

Before looking at data you choose a significance level, α: how often you are prepared to reject a null that is actually true. Convention says 0.05. Convention is all it is.

That gives two distinct failures. A type I error is rejecting a true null — announcing a bent coin that was fair. Its rate is α, by construction. A type II error is failing to reject a false null — shrugging at a coin that really was bent. Its rate is β, and unlike α, you do not get to choose it. It falls out of your sample size and how big the real effect is.

Tightening α to 0.01 buys fewer false alarms and pays for it in misses. Loosening to 0.10 does the reverse. The only way to improve both at once is more data.

One wrinkle worth seeing, because it exposes how arbitrary the threshold is. With a hundred flips at α = 0.05 two-sided, the rejection region is "39 or fewer, or 61 or more." Counts are whole numbers, so the actual type I rate of that test is 3.52%, not 5%. You cannot dial a discrete test to exactly five percent; it lands where the arithmetic puts it. And note the consequence for chapter 01: sixty heads is inside the acceptance region. The exact test does not reject.

Check yourself

You tighten α from 0.05 to 0.01. What happens?

You bought fewer false alarms with more misses. The two errors sit on opposite ends of one lever, and the only thing that moves both the right way is sample size. This is why "we used a stricter threshold" is not automatically a more careful analysis — on a small sample it mostly means finding nothing.
03

Power: the number nobody reports and everybody needs

A hundred flips finds a genuinely 55/45 coin 13.5% of the time. Most negative results are not evidence of nothing.

Power is the chance your test finds a real effect that is really there. It is 1 − β, and it is the most consequential quantity in the whole apparatus because it is the one almost nobody computes.

Take a coin that genuinely lands heads 55% of the time and test it at α = 0.05. With a hundred flips the power is 13.5%. Not 90%, not 50% — thirteen and a half percent. Six times out of seven the test comes back "not significant" on a coin that is genuinely bent.

The numbers going up: 250 flips gives 35.2%, 500 gives 59.0%, 1,000 gives 88.0%. The first sample size that reaches the conventional 80% is 786.

That number comes with a wrinkle, and it is chapter 02’s discreteness arriving again. Power does not rise monotonically with sample size. At 786 flips it is 80.1%; at 787 it drops to 79.2%, and it keeps falling back below the line until 819. Only from 820 is it reliably above. The cause is visible in the rejection region — it only moves when a whole count crosses it, so the realized α sawtooths between about 4.2% and 5.0%, and power sawtooths with it. If you want 80% power, ask for 820 rather than 786. For 90% the first crossing is 1,055 and the reliable point is 1,080.

This reframes what a negative result means. "No significant difference" from an underpowered test is not evidence that there is no difference — it is barely evidence of anything. To read a null result you have to know what effect the test could have caught, and if the answer is "only an enormous one," the result is silence rather than absence.

It also connects straight to module 13. There, an edge clears two standard errors after 4 ÷ Sharpe² years. But merely clearing the bar once is a weaker requirement than having a good chance of clearing it: designing for 80% power needs 7.85 ÷ Sharpe² years, near enough double. A Sharpe of 1.0 crosses t = 2 in four years and takes almost eight before you would reliably expect it to.

Where this comes from

To reject, the observed effect must clear zα/2 standard errors under the null. To have probability 1 − β of doing so when the truth is δ away, the true effect must additionally sit zβ standard errors above that line. So you need δ ≥ (zα/2 + zβ)·σ/√n, and rearranging gives the sample size directly:

n = (zα/2 + zβ)² σ² / δ². At the conventional α = 0.05 and 80% power the bracket is (1.960 + 0.842)² = 7.849, which is where the doubling against t = 2 comes from — 4 becomes 7.85. For a 55/45 coin this formula gives 1,047 flips for 90% power against the exact answer of 1,055, an error under one percent.

Where it breaks: it needs δ, the effect size, which is the thing you do not know — that is why you are running the test. In practice people plug in the smallest effect that would matter to them, which is honest, or the effect their pilot study found, which is not: a pilot that reached significance on a small sample necessarily overstates the effect, so powering off it guarantees an underpowered study. The formula also assumes a known σ and a continuous sampling distribution, so it returns 782.5 where the exact first crossing is 786 — and, worse, it returns a single number where the exact answer is a sawtooth with no single crossing at all.

Check yourself

A study finds no significant difference between two treatments. What have you learned?

You cannot read a negative result without its power. A test of a genuinely 55/45 coin at n = 100 returns "not significant" 86.5% of the time. Reporting that as "the coin is fair" inverts the finding completely, and this is exactly how a literature fills with confident null results that are nothing but small samples.
04

Comparing two things

Two traders, two hit rates, and a gap of eleven points that means nothing at all.

The commonest real question is not "is this different from zero" but "is this different from that." The machinery is the same; only the standard error changes, because now two noisy numbers are being subtracted and their uncertainties add.

Two proportions. Trader A is right on 58 of 100, trader B on 47 of 100. Eleven percentage points looks decisive on a whiteboard. Pool the rates at 52.5%, and the standard error of the difference is 7.06 points — so the gap is 1.56 standard errors, for a p-value of 0.119. Not significant. For an eleven-point gap to clear the bar at these sample sizes it would have to be 13.85 points.

Two means. Two strategies, five years of monthly returns each: 4.8% a month against 2.1%, with volatilities of 21% and 19%. The difference is 2.7 points a month, which annualizes to something a business would be built on. The standard error of that difference is 3.66 points, giving t = 0.74 on 117 degrees of freedom and a p-value of 0.46.

Five years of data, a gap that would double the business, and the honest verdict is that the two are indistinguishable. That is not a defect in the test. It is module 08's point arriving in the place it hurts: returns are so noisy relative to their means that the sample sizes a career provides are simply not large.

Check yourself

Trader A hits 58% and trader B hits 47%, both over 100 trades. Who would you hire?

One hundred trades each cannot separate them. This is the most expensive routine error in the industry: ranking people or strategies on samples far too small to rank them, then acting on the ranking. The gap may well be real — the test simply cannot see it yet, which is chapter 03's point about power arriving in a hiring meeting.
05

When the data are categories

Counts in buckets need their own statistic. It compares what you saw against what you would have expected, and it has one moving part.

Sometimes the data are not measurements but counts falling into categories: which face the die showed, whether the market went up or down. The statistic for those is chi-square, and it is one idea repeated across the buckets — squared gap over expected, summed.

Goodness of fit. A die rolled 300 times gives 43, 55, 62, 48, 51, 41. You expected 50 each. The statistic comes to 6.08 on 5 degrees of freedom, for a p-value of 0.299. Nothing to see — a fair die produces spreads at least this uneven about thirty percent of the time. The 62 that catches your eye is what randomness looks like.

Independence. Now a two-way table. A signal fires on 100 days and does not on another 100. When it fires the market rises 42 times; when it does not, 28. Are the signal and the direction related? The expected counts come from the row and column totals as if they were unrelated, and the statistic is 4.31 on 1 degree of freedom, giving p = 0.038.

One detail worth noticing because it ties the chapter to the last one: for a 2×2 table, chi-square is exactly the square of the two-proportion z-statistic. Here z = 2.0755 and z² = 4.3077, which is the chi-square to four decimals. They are not two tests. They are one test in two notations, which is worth knowing before someone runs both and reports the one they prefer.

χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² ÷ expected

Reads as: add up how far each bucket missed, squared, in units of how much it held. The division is what makes a miss of ten out of an expected twelve matter more than a miss of ten out of an expected five hundred.

Check yourself

A die rolled 300 times gives 43, 55, 62, 48, 51, 41. Is it loaded?

That spread is what fair looks like. Expecting six equal counts is the same instinct module 06 corrects — real randomness is lumpier than people's picture of it, and the largest of six noisy counts is always going to look like it means something.
06

Test twenty things and one will look real

Everything above assumes you ran one test. Nobody runs one test.

A 5% significance level means a true null gets rejected one time in twenty. That is a tolerable rate for one test and a catastrophe for a search.

Run 20 independent tests with nothing real in any of them and the chance at least one comes back significant is 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 64.15%. At 50 tests it is 92.3%; at 100, 99.4%. The expected number of false positives is simply α times the count — one in twenty, five in a hundred. Nothing has gone wrong with the mathematics. The threshold was never a promise about a batch.

The blunt fix is Bonferroni: divide α by the number of tests. For 20 tests that is 0.0025, bringing the family-wise error back to 4.88% and moving the critical z from 1.96 to 3.02. It works, and it is expensive: the power to find a genuinely 55/45 coin on a thousand flips falls from 88.7% to 55.6%.

Now the version that matters here. A researcher tries a hundred strategy variants. Suppose 5% of the things anyone tries actually work, the tests have 80% power, and the false positive rate is the nominal 5%. Of a hundred tried: about 4 real ones are found, and about 4.75 dead ones pass anyway. A strategy that passes is real with probability 45.7% — worse than a coin flip, from a well-run test with no cheating at all.

And that is the optimistic case. Module 10 runs the same arithmetic with a 10% effective false-positive rate — realistic once you account for the variants quietly discarded along the way — and the answer falls to 29.6%. The problem is not that people are dishonest with p-values. It is that a threshold designed for one test is being asked to police a search.

Check yourself

You backtest 20 variants and one comes back with p = 0.04. What do you have?

You found the thing the search was always going to find. The p-value is correct for the test it describes and meaningless as a description of the search that produced it. The honest options are to correct the threshold, to hold out data the search never touched, or — best — to have had a reason for the variant before testing it, which is what raises the prior rather than patching the p-value.

Drill it

Reading a test, then designing one.

The first tier converts between z, χ² and p-values. The second is the harder skill: how much data a question needs before it can be answered at all.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Sixty heads in a hundred flips. What is the exact two-sided p-value, and does it clear the 5% bar?

0.0569 exactly, 0.0455 approximately. Opposite verdicts on identical data. The third option is the one-sided exact value — half the two-sided one — and choosing between one and two sides after seeing which way the data went is its own way of manufacturing significance.
Question 2 · chapter 01

Which statement about a p-value of 0.03 is correct?

It conditions on the null, not on the data. Turning it around into a statement about the hypothesis needs a prior — the whole content of module 10, and the reason the same p-value supports very different conclusions depending on how plausible the claim was to begin with.
Question 3 · chapter 03

A coin genuinely lands heads 55% of the time. Testing 100 flips at α = 0.05, how often does the test catch it?

Thirteen and a half percent. Which means a "not significant" verdict here is almost uninformative — the test was never capable of the job. Power depends on sample size, α and the true effect size, and it is the quantity that goes unreported far more often than any other.
Question 4 · chapter 04

Trader A is right 58 times out of 100, trader B 47 out of 100. Is the difference significant?

p = 0.119, comfortably short. Two noisy numbers are being subtracted and their uncertainties add, so a difference always needs more data than either level did. Ranking traders or strategies on a hundred observations each is ranking noise.
Question 5 · chapter 05

For a 2×2 table, how does chi-square relate to the two-proportion z-test?

One test in two notations. Worth knowing before somebody runs both and reports whichever came out lower. The equivalence is exact for a 2×2 table, not asymptotic.
Question 6 · chapter 06

You test 20 independent hypotheses at α = 0.05 and none of them is real. What is the chance at least one comes back significant?

Just under two-thirds. A 5% threshold is a promise about one test and says nothing about a batch. Bonferroni restores it by dividing α by 20, at a cost: the power to find a real 55/45 coin on a thousand flips drops from 88.7% to 55.6%.

Every test on this page is two-sided, which is the honest default and not always what gets reported — choosing one side after seeing which way the data went halves the p-value for free and is the most common quiet abuse of the method. The power calculations assume the effect size is known, which it never is; in practice the number to plug in is the smallest effect that would change what you do, not the effect a pilot study found, since a pilot that reached significance on a small sample necessarily overstates it. The chi-square tests assume expected counts of roughly five or more in every cell and are unreliable below that, where an exact test belongs instead. The multiple-comparisons arithmetic assumes independent tests; real strategy variants are heavily correlated, which makes the family-wise error lower than the formula says and the effective number of independent tries much harder to count than twenty. And the entire framework answers only "could this have been chance" — never "is this large enough to care about," which is a question no p-value has ever addressed.
