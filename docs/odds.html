Three ways to say one number

A probability, a set of odds and a price are the same object wearing different clothes. Learn to move between them without thinking, then watch what happens when the numbers refuse to add up to one — which they always do.

Scroll ↓

Start here

This is the first page of the series and it assumes nothing — no statistics, no probability, no options. It starts from a single question anyone can answer: what would you pay for a ticket that pays a dollar if a coin lands heads? Everything else on this page, and a fair amount of everything else in the series, is that question asked in harder clothes.

  2. Chapters 01 to 04 are betting. They are the cleanest place to learn this, because a bookmaker's margin is visible arithmetic rather than a spread you have to infer.
  3. Chapters 05 and 06 are the same arithmetic on an option chain — and then the one place where the analogy breaks, which is the most important paragraph on the page.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
pA probability, between 0 and 1. 0.40 and "40%" are the same thing written twice.
Decimal oddsWhat one unit staked returns in total, stake included. 2.50 means you get 2.50 back for 1 risked.
Fractional oddsProfit against stake. 3/1 means three won for one risked, so 4.00 back in total.
American oddsProfit on a 100 stake if positive; the stake needed to win 100 if negative. +150 and −150 are not mirror images.
OverroundHow much more than 100% the implied probabilities add to. The bookmaker's margin, made of arithmetic rather than opinion.

01

A price is a probability, said out loud

Anything that pays exactly one unit if something happens has a price. That price is what someone believes, converted into money.

Here is a ticket. If the coin lands heads it pays you $1. If it lands tails it pays nothing. What is it worth?

Fifty cents, and you did not need any statistics to say so. What you did was multiply: half a chance of a dollar is fifty cents. That multiplication has a name — expected value — and it is the entire foundation of the subject.

Now run it backwards, because that is the direction that matters. Somebody offers you 62 cents for the ticket. They have not told you what they think the coin is; they have told you anyway. A price for a one-unit payoff is a probability. There is nothing else it can be.

That is why this page comes first. Options, betting markets, insurance and prediction markets are all the same machine, and the machine takes beliefs in and hands prices out. Everything in this series is about reading it in the other direction.

price of a $1-if-it-happens ticket = p

Reads as: the price is the probability. Read left to right it prices a belief; read right to left it extracts one from a market. The second direction is the useful one.

Try this

Set the offered price to 62 cents and predict, before you look, what edge you have if the coin really is fair. Then set the payout to $10 and watch what happens to the same edge.

Check yourself

A contract pays $100 if a company is acquired by June and nothing otherwise. It trades at $30. What has the market said?

Thirty percent, and the precision is the point. "Unlikely" cannot be traded against, hedged, or checked afterwards. The whole reason to convert prices into probabilities is that probabilities can be argued with: if you think it is 45%, you now know exactly how much you disagree and exactly what the disagreement is worth. Chapter 06 is about the ways this reading can mislead you, but the reading itself is always available.
02

Three notations, and the one that includes your stake

Fractional, decimal and American odds all say the same thing. Two of them quote profit and one quotes the total return, which is where every conversion error starts.

The same 25% chance is written +300 on a US sportsbook, 4.00 in decimal, and 3/1 in the fractional notation the horse tracks still use. Nothing about the underlying claim differs. What differs is what each notation counts.

American quotes profit on a hundred if the price is a longshot — +300 wins 300 on a 100 stake — and the stake needed to win a hundred if it is a favorite, so −150 risks 150 to win 100. Decimal, which is what every exchange and most models use, quotes the total coming back including your own money: 4.00 means the one you staked plus the three you won. Fractional quotes profit against stake, so 3/1 wins three for one risked.

Decimal is the one that includes your stake, and forgetting that is the single most common error in the whole subject. It is also the notation to think in, because the conversion to a probability is a reciprocal and nothing else.

p = 1 / decimal · p = den / (num + den) · p = 100 / (american + 100)

Reads as: decimal odds invert straight to a probability; the other two need the stake added back first. The American formula shown is the positive branch — a negative price uses |a| / (|a| + 100).

There is one more thing worth noticing, and it is a real limitation rather than a curiosity. American odds are quoted as whole numbers, so they cannot express every probability. Sweep the whole range and the worst rounding error is 0.12 percentage points, and it happens right at the money — near 50%, where a tick of the quote is worth the most. On a market where a percentage point is the entire edge, the notation itself is a source of imprecision.

Try this

Predict what the American quote does as the probability crosses 50% from below. Most people expect it to pass through zero.

Check yourself

A price moves from decimal 2.00 to decimal 1.80. What happened to the implied probability?

Up from 50.0% to 55.6%. The reciprocal reverses the direction, and the size of the move is not the size of the price move either: a 10% cut in the odds is a 5.6-point rise in the probability here, and would be a different number somewhere else on the scale. Any time you are tempted to reason about a percentage change in odds, convert first.
03

The prices add to more than one, always

Two outcomes, one of which must happen, both quoted at −110. Their implied probabilities sum to 104.76%. That extra 4.76% is the entire business model.

Take the simplest market there is. Two outcomes, exactly one will occur, and the book quotes −110 on each side — risk 110 to win 100, either way.

Convert both. Each price implies 52.381%. Add them and you get 104.762%, which is not a probability of anything, because the two outcomes are exhaustive and their probabilities have to sum to one. The overround is the 4.762% left over, and it is not an error — it is the price of using the market.

Read it as a hurdle and it becomes concrete. To break even at −110 you must win 52.381% of the time, which is the implied probability, which is not a coincidence — it is the same number seen from the other end. A bettor who wins 55% of coin flips at that price makes $5.00 per hundred risked. One who wins 52% loses 73 cents, despite being right more often than not.

overround = Σ pᵢ − 1 · breakeven hit rate = the implied probability

Reads as: add up what the prices imply; the excess over one is the margin. And the price you pay is exactly the hit rate you need, which is why a market's own quote tells you the bar you have to clear.

The number that makes this real is how long it takes to know. A bettor with a genuine 55% edge at −110 earns five dollars a bet against a standard deviation of ninety-five. Getting the edge to stand two standard errors clear of zero takes about 1,444 bets. That is not a statistical curiosity — it is why a good bettor and a lucky one are indistinguishable for a very long time, and it is the same arithmetic that makes a volatility desk measure itself in dollar gamma rather than hit rate.

Try this

Push the price to −200 on both sides and predict the breakeven hit rate before you look. Then ask yourself what kind of market quotes that.

Check yourself

You back both sides of a −110 two-way market. What is your outcome?

A guaranteed loss of 4.55% of the stake. This is the cleanest possible demonstration that the overround is real money rather than a bookkeeping artifact — it is the amount you can pay for certainty. It is also the test for whether a market is worth arbitraging: when the implied probabilities sum to less than one, backing everything is free money, which is why that almost never happens and never lasts.
04

Taking the margin back out, and getting it wrong

To learn what a market thinks you must remove the margin. The obvious method is wrong in a specific and predictable direction.

A two-way book quotes 1.20 and 4.50. Raw, those imply 83.333% and 22.222%, summing to 105.556%. To recover what the market believes you have to take 5.556 points out — and how you take them out changes the answer.

The obvious method is to scale everything down proportionally, which gives 78.947% and 21.053%. Clean, fast, and used everywhere. It also assumes the bookmaker applied its margin evenly across both outcomes, and bookmakers do not.

They take more margin on longshots. This is the favorite–longshot bias, and it is one of the oldest documented regularities in betting markets. Fit the margin as a power instead — raise each implied probability to a common exponent chosen so they sum to one — and the same book gives 81.501% and 18.499%. The longshot is 2.554 points lower than proportional said.

proportional: qᵢ = pᵢ / Σpⱼ power: qᵢ = pᵢk, with k solving Σ pⱼk = 1

Reads as: either divide the excess out evenly, or find the exponent that squeezes the set onto one. The exponent is above one whenever there is margin, and it pulls small probabilities down harder than large ones.

Two and a half points does not sound like much until you notice the size of the edges people trade on. On a market where the whole opportunity is two or three points of mispricing, choosing the wrong de-vig method is not a refinement — it manufactures the edge, or hides it. The honest position is that neither method is correct: they are both models of how a book was built, and the right one is an empirical question about the specific book.

Which is the actual lesson, and it recurs for the rest of this series: every number extracted from a price required an assumption, and the assumption is usually invisible. Chapter 05 does the same extraction on an option chain, where the assumption has a name.

Try this

Drag the favorite's price toward even money and watch the two methods converge. Then push it out to 1.05 — a heavy favorite — and see how far apart they get.

Check yourself

Why does it matter which de-vig method you use, if both remove exactly the same total margin?

The distribution, not the total. On an even book they agree exactly; on 1.05 against 11.00 they disagree by several points on the longshot. And the fourth answer is worth dismissing explicitly: proportional de-vigging does sum to one by construction — that is all it guarantees, and summing to one is a much weaker property than being right.
05

The same arithmetic, on an option chain

An option market quotes probabilities too. It calls them deltas and digitals, and its overround is the bid-offer spread.

A stock is at 100. You want the market's probability that it finishes above 110 in three months. There is no line quoted for that, but there is a way to read one.

Buy the 109.5 call and sell the 110.5 call, one point apart, and hold a hundred of them. Above 110.5 that package pays a fixed amount; below 109.5 it pays nothing; in between it ramps. Squeeze the strikes together and it becomes a claim that pays one unit if the stock is above 110 — the ticket from chapter 01, built out of listed options. Its price is 0.2205, and undoing the discounting gives 22.21%.

That number has a name in the options world: it is N(d₂), the second term of Black–Scholes. It is worth knowing that the formula's most opaque-looking piece is just an implied probability, and that you can get to it from a betting page without ever writing the formula down.

In betting notation, 22.21% is decimal 4.502, or American +350.

tight call spread, scaled by 1/width → e−rT · N(d₂)

Reads as: squeeze a call spread and you get a bet on finishing above the strike. The discount factor is there because options pay at expiry and bets are settled then too — the price is today's money for tomorrow's dollar.

The overround survives the translation intact. Quote that digital two cents wide — bid 0.2105, offer 0.2305 — and buy both sides: 0.2305 for "yes" and 0.7820 for "no". You have paid 1.0125 for something that certainly returns 0.9925, which is an overround of 2.02%. It is a bid-offer spread rather than a bookmaker's margin, and it does exactly the same job, and it is charged the same way: on both sides, whichever wins.

Try this

Move the strike down to 100 and predict the implied probability before you look. Most people say fifty percent.

Check yourself

Why is the at-the-money implied probability not exactly 50%?

Drift and the shape of the distribution, pulling in opposite directions. The financing rate pushes the whole distribution right, which raises the probability of finishing above the spot; the lognormal's right skew puts the median below the mean, which lowers it. Which one wins depends on the volatility and the horizon, and that competition is the σ²/2 in every option formula you will ever meet.
06

Where a price stops being a probability

The most important paragraph on this page, and it undoes some of the preceding five.

Everything so far has treated a price as a belief. For a coin ticket that is exactly right. For anything anyone actually trades, it is right only after a correction, and the correction is not small.

Take the same stock at 100 and ask the real question: how often does it actually finish above 110 in a year? The options market quotes the number computed with a drift equal to the financing rate — 34.97%. But a stock is not a bank deposit; if you believe equities drift up at 8%, the honest probability is 42.61%, and at 12% it is 48.95%. Same volatility, same strike, same horizon. Fourteen points of difference, entirely from an assumption nobody quoted.

The market's number is not wrong. It is a risk-neutral probability: the weighting that makes prices consistent with each other and with the cost of hedging. It is the right number for pricing and the wrong number for forecasting, and the gap between the two is a risk premium — what people charge for holding something they might not want.

The same thing happens in betting, without the vocabulary. An implied 22% on a longshot is not a claim that it wins 22% of the time; it is the price at which the book is willing to take the other side, given what it costs to be wrong. Whenever you read a probability out of a price, you are reading a probability plus a premium for someone's discomfort, and separating those two is most of the work.

Check yourself

The options market implies a 35% chance the stock finishes above 110. You believe it is 43%. Is that an edge?

Not by itself, and this is the distinction that separates a trader from a punter. A stock's expected return above the risk-free rate is a risk premium you can earn by owning the stock — you do not need options to collect it, and buying upside digitals is an expensive way to try. A genuine options edge is a disagreement about volatility or shape, not about drift, because those are the things the price is actually a bet on.
07

Drill it until the conversions are free

These four conversions need to be automatic before anything later in the series will feel easy.

Everything on this page rests on moving between a price, a probability and a set of odds without stopping to think. That is not understanding, it is fluency, and fluency only comes from repetition. Two tiers: the conversions themselves, then the margin arithmetic on top of them.

Six questions across the page.

Question 1 · chapter 01

A contract pays $1 if it rains tomorrow. It trades at 0.35. What is the market saying?

35%. The payout is known — one unit — which is what makes the reading immediate. When the payout is something else, divide by it first; that is the only extra step.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Decimal odds of 4.00. What are the fractional and American equivalents?

3/1 and +300. The single most common conversion error in the subject is carrying the stake across. Decimal 4.00 returns four for one risked, of which three is profit, and profit is what the other two notations quote.
Question 3 · chapter 03

Both sides of a market are quoted at −110. What hit rate do you need to break even?

52.381%, and that it equals the implied probability is not a coincidence. A price is a break-even hit rate seen from the other side. Winning 52% of the time at this price loses 73 cents per hundred risked, which is why "I win more than half my bets" is not a claim about profitability.
Question 4 · chapter 03

A soccer match is quoted at 2.10, 3.40 and 3.80 — home, draw, away. What is the overround?

3.35%. Exhaustive outcomes are exactly why the sum is diagnostic: it must be 100% for real probabilities, so whatever it exceeds that by is margin. Do not round the parts before adding them — the whole quantity you are after lives in the third decimal place.
Question 5 · chapter 04

A book quotes 1.20 and 4.50. Proportional de-vigging gives 78.9% and 21.1%. What is the objection?

Even distribution is an assumption, and a documented-wrong one. Both methods sum to one; that is all "sums to one" buys you. On a market where the whole edge is two or three points, the choice of method can manufacture the opportunity you think you found.
Question 6 · chapter 06

Options imply a 35% chance of finishing above 110; you think it is 43%. What have you found?

A risk premium wearing a mispricing's clothes. The market's number is computed at the financing rate by construction, so it will nearly always sit below what an equity investor believes. The fourth answer is too strong: the numbers can be compared, and the comparison is exactly how you measure the premium — it just is not free money.

Every price on this page is a clean quote with no size attached, which is the assumption that flatters this arithmetic most. A real book moves as you hit it, a real option chain is only tradeable in the size shown, and the overround you can actually pay is wider than the one you can compute from a screen. The de-vigging in chapter 04 assumes the margin follows one of two simple shapes; both are models, neither is the truth, and the right one is an empirical question about the specific book. Chapter 05's chain is priced on a flat volatility surface, which makes the implied probabilities cleaner than any real chain's — a real skew changes them, and changes them most in the tails where the longshots live.
