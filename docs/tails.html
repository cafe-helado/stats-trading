The last one percent is the whole job

Everything before this module assumed the middle of the distribution was the interesting part. Five days out of ten years carry fourteen percent of a decade's variance, the standard risk measure can get worse when you diversify, and the average of a fat-tailed thing may not mean anything at all.

Scroll ↓

Start here

This is the last of the foundations and the one that argues with the others. Modules 03 and 05 leaned on the normal distribution and on averages; this one is about where both stop working, and what people use instead.

  1. Chapter 01 is the fact to carry. Over ten years of returns, the worst five days hold about 14% of the total squared return and the worst 1% of days hold about 31%. Risk is not spread evenly across time.
  2. Chapter 03 is the one that surprises risk managers. Value at risk is not subadditive: two independent positions can have a combined VaR larger than the sum of theirs, so the number gets worse when you diversify.
  3. Chapter 05 is the honest ending. The failure mode is never the model's middle. It is that a tail parameter was estimated from a sample containing no tail.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
SkewnessWhether the long side of the distribution is the left or the right. Zero for anything symmetric.
Excess kurtosisHow much of the variance comes from the extremes, scaled so a normal reads zero.
VaRValue at risk: the loss you exceed a stated fraction of the time. A quantile, and nothing more.
Expected shortfallThe average loss given that you exceeded the VaR. Answers "how bad when it is bad."
SubadditiveA risk measure where combining positions never increases the total. Expected shortfall is; VaR is not.
Tail indexHow fast a tail decays as a power law. Below 2 the variance is infinite; below 1 the mean is.
Backtest exceptionA day whose loss exceeded the model's VaR. Counting them is how a risk model is judged.
ConcentrationThe share of total variance carried by the worst handful of observations.
01

A decade of risk lives in a handful of days

Sort ten years of returns by size and look at how quickly the total accumulates. It is not gradual.

Take ten years of daily returns — 2,520 observations — and rank them by absolute size. Then ask how much of the total squared return, which is what variance is made of, the biggest few contribute.

The single worst day carries about 3.3% of a decade's variance. The worst five days — one fifth of one percent of the sample — carry about 14%. The worst 25 days, one percent of them, carry about 31%. The worst 5% of days carry 54% — more than half a decade of risk in about six months' worth of sessions.

Nothing exotic produced that. The returns here come from a two-regime mixture like module 04's — normal 94% of the time and turbulent the rest — which is about as mild a departure from normality as it is possible to construct.

Two consequences follow immediately. Any risk estimate computed from a window that happens to exclude those days is not slightly low, it is wrong by a third. And any strategy whose returns come from being present on ordinary days and absent on extreme ones is being measured against the wrong denominator.

Check yourself

A ten-year volatility estimate is computed from a window that happens to miss the worst 25 days. How wrong is it?

A third of the variance, from one percent of the sample. Which is why volatility estimates drop so far during calm stretches and why they are least reliable exactly when they look most stable. It is also the argument for using a long window even though the recent one forecasts better — the long window is the only one likely to contain a tail.
02

Value at risk, and the question it refuses to answer

VaR tells you the threshold you cross 1% of the time. It says nothing about how far past it you go.

Value at risk is a quantile. The 99% one-day VaR is the loss you exceed on one day in a hundred, and under a normal distribution it sits at 2.326 standard deviations.

The complaint is not that it is wrong; it is that it is silent about everything past the threshold. Two books can have identical VaR and completely different behavior on the days that breach it — one loses slightly more, the other loses everything.

Expected shortfall answers the missing question: given that you breached, how bad is it on average? Under a normal at the 99% level it is 2.665 standard deviations, a ratio of 1.15 to the VaR. That ratio is the useful number, because it is the one that changes with the tail shape — under fatter tails, VaR barely moves and expected shortfall moves a lot.

On a $10 million book with 1.26% daily volatility: 99% VaR of $293,000 and an expected shortfall of $336,000. The second number is what you should be sizing against.

VaRp = the p-quantile of losses ESp = E[ loss | loss > VaRp ]

Reads as: one is where the tail starts and the other is the average of what is in it. Two books can match on the first and differ by any amount on the second.

Check yourself

Two books have the same 99% VaR. What do you know about their risk?

Nothing about the tail. A book that is short far out-of-the-money options can be engineered to have a small VaR at any confidence level — you simply place the strikes past the quantile — while its expected shortfall is enormous. That is not a hypothetical; it is a known way to game a risk limit, and it is a large part of why regulators moved to expected shortfall.
03

Diversify, and the risk number goes up

Two independent positions, each with a 95% VaR of zero. Put them together and it is 100. That is not a bug in the arithmetic.

Here is the cleanest demonstration that VaR is not a coherent measure of risk. Two bonds, independent, each defaulting with probability 3% and losing 100 when it does.

The 95% VaR of one bond is zero. The loss is 100 only 3% of the time, and 3% is inside the 5% tail you are ignoring, so the 95th percentile of the loss distribution is zero. Both bonds, separately, report no risk.

Now hold both. The chance that at least one defaults is 1 − 0.97² = 5.91%, which is more than 5%. So the 95th percentile of the combined loss is 100, and the portfolio's VaR is 100 > 0 + 0.

Diversifying made the reported risk go up. This is subadditivity failing, and it is not a rounding artifact — it is structural, and it happens whenever losses are lumpy near the quantile you chose.

Expected shortfall does not do this. On the same pair: 60 for each bond alone and 101.8 for the pair, which is comfortably under the sum of 120. Expected shortfall is subadditive by construction, and that property is the main technical reason the Basel framework moved to it.

Check yourself

Why does expected shortfall avoid the problem that VaR has here?

Averaging is linear; taking a quantile is not. That single difference gives expected shortfall subadditivity for free, and it is why the property is provable rather than empirical. The cost is that expected shortfall is harder to estimate — it depends on the shape of a tail you have few observations of, which is chapter 05's problem.
04

Measuring the shape you are exposed to

Two numbers describe a tail: which side it is on, and how heavy it is. Both are unstable in exactly the situations where they matter.

Skewness says which side the long tail is on. Equity index returns are negatively skewed — the crashes are bigger than the rallies — and short-option strategies are extremely so, which is the shape that makes them feel good for years and then not.

Excess kurtosis says how much of the variance is in the extremes. A normal reads zero; daily equity returns typically read between 3 and 8; a single crisis in a sample can double it.

The catch is in that last clause. Skewness is a third moment and kurtosis a fourth, so both are dominated by the largest few observations. A sample can produce a kurtosis of 2 in its first half and 9 in its second because one bad week landed in the second — and the honest response is not to average them but to notice that a fourth moment estimated from a few hundred observations barely has a value.

The more robust way to describe a tail is to fit how fast it decays. If the probability of a loss larger than x falls like x−α, then α is the tail index, and it has a striking property: the variance is infinite when α < 2, and the mean is infinite when α < 1. Estimates of α for equity returns cluster around 3 to 5 — comfortably finite, but close enough to the boundary that the fourth moment, which needs α > 4, is genuinely borderline. Which is a formal way of saying kurtosis estimates for financial returns may not converge at all.

Check yourself

Why is a tail index more useful than kurtosis for describing extreme risk?

It survives where moments do not. If the tail index is below 4 the kurtosis is infinite in the population, so any sample kurtosis you compute is an artifact of the sample size rather than an estimate of anything. The tail index remains well defined all the way down, which is why extreme value theory works in α rather than in moments.
05

What actually breaks

Not the model's middle. A tail parameter estimated from a sample containing no tail.

Every module in this series has pointed at the same structural failure from a different angle, and it is worth stating once directly.

The data that would validate a tail model does not exist in the sample used to fit it. Two years of daily returns is about 500 observations. A 99% VaR should be breached five times in it; a 99.9% level should be breached once. There is no amount of statistical technique that turns five observations into a reliable estimate of the shape of a tail, and none that turns one observation into anything at all.

So tail parameters get set by one of three routes, all of which have failure modes worth naming. Extrapolate a parametric family — fast, and wrong by two orders of magnitude if the family is wrong, as module 04 showed with a mixture that is thinner at two sigma and 116 times fatter at four. Use a longer history — better, and it assumes the world has not changed. Use a stress scenario — honest about being a judgment, and no longer a probability statement at all.

The practical posture that follows is not despair, it is asymmetry. Be precise about the middle, where you have data. Be conservative about the tail, where you do not. And size positions so that being wrong about the tail is survivable — which is module 02's argument for fractional Kelly, arriving from the other direction.

Check yourself

A 99% VaR model produces two exceptions in a year of daily data instead of the expected 2.5. What have you learned?

Nothing distinguishable. This is the structural problem with backtesting tail models: at the confidence levels people care about, the number of observations that carry information is tiny, and the Poisson noise in that count swamps the difference between a right model and a wrong one. It takes years of data to reject a bad 99% model and decades to reject a bad 99.9% one.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Over ten years of fat-tailed daily returns, roughly what share of the total variance sits in the worst 1% of days?

Roughly a third, from one percent of the sample. Which is why a volatility estimated from a window that missed those days is wrong by a third in variance and about 17% in volatility, and why calm-period estimates are least reliable exactly when they look most stable.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Under a normal distribution, where does the 99% VaR sit?

2.326, with expected shortfall 15% further out. The fourth answer is the 95% VaR. Both multipliers are worth memorizing, and so is the ratio between VaR and expected shortfall — it is the number that moves most when the tail is not normal.
Question 3 · chapter 02

What does expected shortfall tell you that VaR does not?

The average of what is past the threshold. Two books with identical VaR can behave completely differently beyond it — and a book that is short far out-of-the-money options can be built to have a small VaR at any chosen level while its expected shortfall is enormous.
Question 4 · chapter 03

Two independent bonds each default 3% of the time losing 100. What are the 95% VaRs, separately and together?

Zero, zero, and 100. Each bond's 3% default sits inside the 5% tail VaR ignores, but the pair defaults at least once 5.91% of the time, which crosses the threshold. Diversifying raised the reported risk — a structural failure of quantiles, not an arithmetic slip.
Question 5 · chapter 04

A tail decays like x−α with α = 3. What exists?

Mean and variance yes, fourth moment no. Which matters practically: equity tail indices are estimated around 3 to 5, so the kurtosis everyone quotes is at or past the boundary of existing. A sample kurtosis in that regime is a function of how much data you collected rather than an estimate of a population quantity.
Question 6 · chapter 05

A 99% VaR model shows two exceptions in a year instead of the expected 2.5. What does that establish?

Nothing distinguishable. At the confidence levels people care about, the informative observations are so few that Poisson noise swamps the signal — it takes years to reject a bad 99% model and decades for a 99.9% one. Which is the argument for sizing positions so that being wrong about the tail is survivable, rather than for estimating it more precisely.

The return series on this page is a two-regime mixture rather than real market data, chosen because it is reproducible and because it is a mild departure from normality — real equity returns are worse, with volatility clustering that makes extreme days arrive together rather than independently, which concentrates the variance further than anything shown here. Every VaR and expected shortfall figure quoted in sigmas assumes a normal distribution and is therefore optimistic; the point of quoting them is the ratio between the two measures rather than either level. The tail index discussion treats α as a fixed property, and estimates of it are notoriously unstable — different estimators on the same data routinely disagree by a full unit, which is enough to move the fourth moment from existing to not. And the subadditivity counterexample uses independent defaults; real credit portfolios are correlated, which makes the problem worse rather than better.
