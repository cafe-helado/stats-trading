An interactive derivation · No. 03

Add enough of anything

Sum up enough independent small things and you always get the same curve — no matter what you started with. Watch it happen from coin flips, then take the one consequence that runs through all of finance: spread grows with the square root of time, and never with time.

Start here

This is the module where the bell curve stops being a thing you were told about and becomes a thing you watched appear. It arrives out of coin flips, then out of dice, then out of a distribution deliberately built to look nothing like it — and the point is that the starting shape does not matter.

  1. Chapter 02 is the payoff. Add five draws from a flat distribution and you are already close to normal; add thirty and nothing short of a formal test can tell the difference.
  2. Chapter 03 fixes a confusion almost everyone carries. As you add more flips the count of heads spreads out and the proportion of heads tightens. Both are true, both are √n, and mixing them up is the source of most bad intuitions about sample size.
  3. Chapter 04 is the one you will use tomorrow. Converting an annual volatility to a daily one divides by √252, not by 252 — a factor of 15.87, which is the single most common units error in finance.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
nHow many independent things you are adding — flips, days, draws.
BinomialThe distribution of how many successes in n independent trials. Coin flips, free throws, assignments.
μ, σThe mean and standard deviation of whatever you are summing.

01

One coin is boring. A hundred is a shape.

Flip one coin and there are two outcomes. Flip a hundred and the outcomes arrange themselves into something with a middle and two tails.

A single fair coin has nothing to say: heads or tails, half each, no structure. Two coins already have a middle — one head is twice as likely as zero or two, because there are two ways to get it. That counting is the whole engine.

Ten flips and the shape is visible. The most likely single result is five heads, at 24.6%, and the chance of seven or more is 17.2%. A hundred flips and it is unmistakably a bell: the mean is 50, the standard deviation is exactly 5, and getting sixty or more heads happens 2.84% of the time.

Nothing has been assumed here. The curve is a consequence of counting how many ways each total can happen, and the reason it looks like the normal distribution is chapter 02.

P(k heads in n flips) = C(n,k) · pᵏ · (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ mean = np, sd = √(np(1−p))

Reads as: count the ways it can happen, times the probability of any one way. The C(n,k) is the counting; the rest is one particular sequence's chance.

Try this

Set the coin to 70% heads and watch what happens to the shape. Predict first whether it stays symmetric.

Check yourself

A hundred fair flips. What is the standard deviation of the number of heads?

Five heads. Which is a useful anchor: on a hundred flips, 60 heads is two standard deviations out and 65 is three. The fourth answer is the per-flip standard deviation, and confusing the two is exactly the mistake chapter 03 exists to prevent.
02

It does not matter what you started with

Sum five draws from a perfectly flat distribution and it is already nearly normal. Sum thirty and nothing you can see will tell them apart.

Coins are suspiciously convenient — symmetric, two outcomes, obviously heading somewhere symmetric. So take the flattest thing available instead: a number drawn uniformly between 0 and 1, which has no peak at all. Add several and standardize.

One draw is a rectangle, as far from a bell as a distribution gets: its excess kurtosis is −1.20. Two draws make a triangle, at −0.59. Five draws reach −0.24, and the largest gap between its cumulative distribution and a normal's is 0.007 — under a percentage point, anywhere. Thirty draws sit at −0.05 with a maximum gap of 0.003.

Nothing about the rectangle survives. This is the central limit theorem, and it is the reason the normal distribution turns up everywhere: most quantities you measure are secretly sums of many small independent things, and sums forget their ingredients.

Try this

Switch the ingredient to the lopsided one and predict how many draws it takes to look normal. Skew takes longer to wash out than flatness does.

Check yourself

The central limit theorem needs one condition that gets left out of most summaries. Which?

Finite variance, and it is not a technicality. The other three are all false: the theorem works for non-identical and asymmetric ingredients, and "thirty" is a rule of thumb rather than a condition. But a distribution with infinite variance — the Cauchy is the standard example — sums to itself forever and never becomes normal. Module 08 is about how close real markets get to that boundary.
03

The count spreads out; the proportion tightens

Both are true at once and they point opposite ways. Almost every wrong intuition about sample size lives in the gap.

Flip a hundred coins and the number of heads has a standard deviation of 5. Flip ten thousand and it is 50. The count of heads becomes more variable as you flip more — it spreads out with √n.

Now measure the same experiment as a percentage. At a hundred flips the proportion of heads has a standard deviation of 5.0 percentage points. At ten thousand it is 0.5 points. The proportion becomes less variable, tightening with 1/√n.

These are the same fact seen through two lenses, and they are both correct. The absolute deviation from the expected count grows; the deviation as a fraction of the total shrinks. Anyone who has ever argued that a long streak "must even out" is confusing them — the count does not come back, it just gets swamped.

sd of the count = √(n·p·q) sd of the proportion = √(p·q/n)

Reads as: the count's spread grows like √n and the proportion's shrinks like 1/√n. One divided by the other is exactly n, which is the whole relationship.

Check yourself

A fair coin has come up heads 10 more times than tails after 100 flips. After 10,000 flips, what do you expect that gap to be?

Bigger in heads and smaller in percent, and there is no force pulling it back. Nothing corrects an early imbalance; it simply stops mattering as the denominator grows. The gambler's fallacy is exactly this confusion — expecting the count to return to zero because the proportion returns to a half.
04

Which makes a month 29% of a year, not 8%

The single most common units error in finance, and it is off by a factor of nearly sixteen at the daily horizon.

Variances add over independent periods. Standard deviations do not — they add in quadrature, which is a fancy way of saying you add the squares and take the root at the end. That one fact sets every conversion between time horizons.

A stock quoted at 20% annual volatility has a daily standard deviation of 20% ÷ √252 = 1.26%. Divide by 252 instead, as if volatility accrued evenly like interest, and you get 0.079% — 15.87 times too small. On a $100 stock that is the difference between expecting daily moves of $1.26 and expecting them of eight cents.

Going the other way is the same arithmetic. A month is 1/12 of a year, so monthly volatility is 20% × √(1/12) = 5.77%, not 1.67%. A quarter is exactly half of a year in volatility terms, because √(1/4) = 1/2.

σ over a period T = σ_annual · √T

Reads as: scale volatility by the square root of the time, never by the time. The three worth memorizing: a month is 0.29, a quarter is 0.50, a day is 0.063.

Try this

Set the horizon to four years and predict the ratio between the two curves before you look. It is not four.

Check yourself

A stock's daily moves have a standard deviation of 2%. What is its annualized volatility?

31.7%, using 252 trading days rather than 365 calendar ones. The day count matters more than people expect: using 365 would give 38.2%, which is a fifth too high. Markets are closed on weekends and prices do not move when nobody is trading, so volatility is counted in trading days even when everything else is counted in calendar days.
05

68, 95, 99.7 — and what the last one really means

The rule everyone half-remembers, made exact, and then pushed out to where it stops describing anything real.

For a normal distribution, 68.27% of the mass sits within one standard deviation of the mean, 95.45% within two, and 99.73% within three. Those are worth knowing exactly rather than approximately, because the interesting quantity is always what is left over.

Outside one sigma is 31.7% — about one day in three. Outside two is 4.55%, one day in 22. Outside three is 0.27%, one in 370, which is roughly once every year and a half of trading.

Keep going and the numbers stop being believable. A four-sigma day should occur once in 15,780 trading days — about once every 63 years. A five-sigma day, once every 6,911 years. Markets deliver both several times a decade, which does not mean the arithmetic is wrong. It means the normal distribution is the wrong model for the tails, and that discrepancy is the subject of module 08.

Check yourself

Under a normal distribution, how often should a four-sigma daily move happen?

Once every 63 years, and the fourth answer is the five-sigma number. Both are wildly at odds with what markets actually deliver, which is the honest conclusion to take away: the normal distribution is an excellent description of the middle of a return distribution and a terrible one of its edges. Using it for ordinary risk is fine; using it for tail risk is how firms fail.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Ten fair coin flips. What is the chance of exactly five heads?

Under a quarter. "Most likely" and "likely" are different claims, and the gap grows with n: on a hundred flips the most likely count is fifty and it happens just 7.96% of the time. The mode of a spread-out distribution is not where the mass is.
Question 2 · chapter 02

You sum five draws from a perfectly flat distribution. How close to normal is the result?

Close enough that you could not tell by eye. The fourth answer inverts the truth and is the important one to reject: convergence is fastest in the middle and slowest in the tails, which is precisely why normal approximations are safe for typical outcomes and dangerous for extreme ones.
Question 3 · chapter 03

Going from 100 flips to 10,000, what happens to the spread of the head count?

Both, depending on which quantity you name. A hundred times the flips is ten times the count-spread and a tenth of the proportion-spread, because √100 = 10. Almost every argument about whether small samples are "enough" turns on which of these the arguer has in mind.
Question 4 · chapter 04

A stock is quoted at 20% annual volatility. What is its daily standard deviation?

1.26%, and the first answer is wrong by a factor of 15.87. Dividing by the number of periods instead of its square root is the most common units error in finance, and it is not a rounding difference — it is an order of magnitude. Variances add; standard deviations do not.
Question 5 · chapter 04

Quarterly volatility, as a fraction of annual?

Half. Worth keeping as the sanity check on every other conversion: if your scaling does not turn a quarter into a half, you have used time instead of its square root somewhere.
Question 6 · chapter 05

How much of a normal distribution lies more than two standard deviations from the mean?

4.55%, and the 5% everyone remembers is the 1.96-sigma figure. The two get used interchangeably and the difference matters when you are counting rare events: 1 in 22 versus 1 in 20 is a 10% error in how often you expect to be surprised.

Every simulation here uses a seeded generator, so the numbers in the prose are the numbers on the screen and will be the same tomorrow. The central limit theorem needs finite variance and independence, and financial returns violate the second one routinely — volatility clusters, so a big day makes the next day bigger, and sums of dependent draws converge more slowly than these figures suggest. The √T rule inherits that assumption whole: it is exactly right for independent increments and progressively wrong as returns become autocorrelated, which is why realized volatility measured over overlapping windows behaves differently from the tidy arithmetic on this page. And the tail probabilities in chapter 05 are the normal's, which is the model this series spends module 08 arguing against.
