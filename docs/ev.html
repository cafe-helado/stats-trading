The average of a thing you do once

Expected value is the most useful number in the subject and the most misleading. It tells you what a bet is worth and nothing about what will happen — and the gap between those is where bet sizing, drawdowns and most blown-up accounts live.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Module 03 turned prices into probabilities. This one asks what to do with a probability once you have it: what a bet is worth, how differently two bets with identical worth can feel, how long it takes to find out whether you were right, and how much of your money to put on it.

  1. Chapters 01 and 02 are a pair. Two roulette bets with exactly the same house edge and standard deviations 5.8× apart. Expectation is what a bet is worth; variance is what it is like to make.
  2. Chapter 03 is the uncomfortable one. A genuine 55% bettor is still behind after a hundred bets three times in ten. Nothing is wrong; that is just what the arithmetic says.
  3. Chapters 04 to 06 are bet sizing, which is the practical payoff of the whole module. Kelly, why double Kelly makes exactly nothing, and why almost nobody who knows the formula uses it at full size.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
E[X]Expected value: every outcome multiplied by its probability, added up. What the bet is worth.
Var, σVariance and its square root, the standard deviation. How far outcomes typically land from the average.
EdgeExpected profit per dollar risked. A 5% edge means every dollar risked returns an average of five cents.
bNet odds received on a win — the profit per dollar staked. A bet at −110 has b = 100/110 = 0.909.
p, qThe chance of winning and of losing, q = 1 − p.
f*The Kelly fraction: the share of the bankroll that maximizes long-run growth.

DrawdownHow far the bankroll falls below its high-water mark. The number that decides whether a strategy is survivable.
01

Expectation is a weighted average, and nothing more

Every outcome times its probability, added up. The whole idea fits on one line, and the line is worth building by hand once.

An American roulette wheel has 38 pockets: 1 through 36, plus a zero and a double zero. Bet a dollar on a single number and it pays 35 to 1. That sounds generous until you count the pockets.

You win with probability 1/38 and collect $35. You lose with probability 37/38 and give up $1. Multiply and add: (1/38)(35) + (37/38)(−1) = −0.0526. Every dollar you put on the table is worth about 94.7 cents the moment you put it there, and the missing 5.26 cents is the house edge — which is exactly the two green pockets, expressed as money.

That is the entire concept. Expectation is arithmetic on a payoff table, and building the table is most of the work; the multiplication is trivial once the table is honest. The hard part in practice is never the sum, it is remembering an outcome you did not list.

E[X] = Σ (outcome × its probability)

Reads as: list every way it can end, weight each by how likely it is, add them up. Anything you forget to list is silently weighted at zero, which is the only way this goes wrong.

Check yourself

A single-number roulette bet has an expected value of −5.26 cents per dollar. What does that predict about your next spin?

It predicts nothing about one spin, which is exactly why it is useful. Expected value is a statement about the aggregate of many repetitions, and single outcomes are the only thing anyone experiences. Holding both ideas at once — the bet is worth −5.26 cents, and the next spin will be +35 or −1 — is the whole skill, and the rest of this page is what goes wrong when people hold only one.
02

Same edge, completely different life

Straight-up and red-black have identical house edges. Their standard deviations differ by a factor of 5.8, and that is what you actually feel.

Bet a dollar on red instead. You win with probability 18/38 and collect a dollar; you lose with probability 20/38. Expected value: −0.0526. Precisely the same as the single number, to every decimal place, because the house edge on an American wheel comes from the two green pockets and applies to every bet on the layout.

Now look at the standard deviations. Red-black is $1.00. Straight-up is $5.76 — 5.8 times larger. Two bets, identical worth, and one of them will bankrupt you in an evening while the other bleeds you slowly enough to feel like entertainment.

This is the first genuinely important idea in the module. Expectation tells you what a bet is worth; variance tells you what it is like to hold. Everything about position sizing, drawdown and risk of ruin lives in the second number, and the first number cannot see it at all.

Var(X) = Σ (outcome − E[X])² × its probability

Reads as: average of the squared distances from the mean. Squaring is what makes big misses dominate — one outcome twice as far away counts four times as much, which is why a single fat tail can set a whole variance.

Try this

Predict what happens to the two paths when you raise the number of spins to 5,000. Does the gap between them widen or narrow, in dollars and in percent?

Check yourself

Which roulette bet should a player who wants to walk out ahead prefer?

The high-variance bet, and the logic inverts once the edge does. With a negative edge, time is the enemy and noise is the only friend — the way to walk out ahead is one big swing before the average catches up. With a positive edge everything reverses: you want as many small bets as possible, because then the average is working for you and noise is the only thing that can beat you. Which is why casinos love high-variance bettors and professional bettors love volume.
03

The long run is longer than anyone thinks

A genuine 55% bettor is still behind after a hundred bets almost a third of the time. Nothing is broken; that is what the arithmetic says.

Take a bettor with a real edge: they win 55% of the time at −110, which is worth $5.00 per hundred risked. That is an excellent number — better than almost anyone actually achieves. How long before it shows?

The single-bet standard deviation is $94.98, roughly nineteen times the edge. Averaging over n bets divides that by √n, so the noise falls slowly and the edge does not fall at all. After 100 bets the standard error is still $9.50 against a $5.00 edge, and the chance of being behind is 29.9%. After 1,000 bets it is 4.8%. After ten thousand it is effectively zero.

Read that middle number again. Three seasons of serious volume, a genuine and large edge, and there is a one-in-twenty chance you are still down. Anyone judging the strategy on that sample would conclude it does not work, and they would be wrong, and they would have no way to tell.

standard error = σ / √n ⟹ n ≈ (z · σ / edge)²

Reads as: noise falls with the square root of the sample, so cutting it in half costs four times the bets. Rearranged, the number of bets needed to prove an edge grows with the square of how small the edge is.

That last rearrangement is worth carrying. Halving the edge does not double the sample you need — it quadruples it. A 5% edge needs about 1,444 bets to stand two standard errors clear of zero. A 2.5% edge needs about 5,800. A 1% edge needs 36,000, which for most people is a career.

Set the hit rate to 53% — still a real edge at this price — and see what happens to the sample you need. Predict whether it doubles or worse.

Check yourself

A bettor is down after 200 wagers. What have you learned?

Almost nothing, and the fourth answer is the interesting wrong one. The sample is absolutely worth collecting — it just has to be combined with a prior rather than read on its own, which is module 10. Two hundred bets is real evidence; it is simply much weaker evidence than it feels, and the feeling is the problem.
04

How much to bet, when money compounds

Maximizing expected profit says bet everything. That answer is wrong, and the reason is that you keep playing with what is left.

You have an edge. How much of the bankroll goes on it? Maximize expected profit and the answer is all of it, every time, because expectation is linear: twice the stake is twice the expected profit, forever. Follow that advice and you are broke on the first loss.

The flaw is that money compounds. What you have after a sequence of bets is a product of multipliers, not a sum, and the quantity that governs a product is the average of the logarithms. Maximize that instead and a finite answer appears:

f* = (b·p − q) / b

Reads as: bet the edge divided by the odds. With b the net odds on a win, p the win chance and q = 1 − p, the numerator b·p − q is exactly the expected profit per dollar staked.

Run it on the 55% bettor at −110. The net odds are b = 100/110 = 0.909, so f* = (0.909 × 0.55 − 0.45) / 0.909 = 0.055. Five and a half percent of the bankroll — no more, no matter how confident. At even money the same 55% edge would justify 10%, and at 2-to-1 it would justify 32.5%: the better the price, the more you can risk, because a win goes further toward repairing the losses.

The growth rate this buys is 0.138% per bet. That sounds tiny and it compounds. The median wealth after n bets is en·g, so a thousand bets is e1.378 — a median multiple of about 4×.

Try this

Push the price out to +200 while holding the win rate at 55%, and predict what happens to the optimal stake before you look.

Check yourself

Why does maximizing expected profit give the wrong stake?

Products, not sums. If your results added, betting everything every time would be correct and there would be no such thing as bet sizing. They multiply, one zero ends the sequence permanently, and the average of a product is dominated by its worst factors. Kelly is what falls out of taking that seriously — nothing more exotic than noticing that you keep playing with what is left.
05

Bet twice Kelly and you make exactly nothing

Not less. Nothing. The growth rate returns to zero at precisely double the optimal stake, and goes negative past it.

The hill in the last figure has a property worth committing to memory. Staking double the Kelly fraction produces a long-run growth rate of zero — you have an edge, you are betting into it, and you compound at nothing.

Check it on the 55% bettor. Kelly is 5.5% and the growth rate is 0.1379% per bet. At 11.0% the growth rate is −0.002%: zero to three decimal places, and negative after that. Every dollar of edge has been converted into variance drag.

The asymmetry is the practical lesson. Betting half Kelly keeps 74.9% of the growth. Betting double keeps none of it. The penalty for timidity is mild and the penalty for aggression is total, so when you are unsure of your edge — and you are always unsure of your edge — the error to make is the small one.

And that is before noticing the other half of the problem: your estimate of p is itself uncertain. If you think you are a 55% bettor and you are really 53%, full Kelly on the wrong number is already overbetting.

g(f) = p·ln(1 + b·f) + q·ln(1 − f)

Reads as: the long-run growth rate is the average of the log multipliers. Maximize it and you get Kelly; set it to zero and the other root is exactly 2f* for small edges, which is where the double-Kelly rule comes from.

Check yourself

Over 1,000 bets the double-Kelly bettor sometimes finishes with more than the Kelly bettor. Does that vindicate the aggression?

The median is the honest summary, and the fourth answer overstates it. Double Kelly does not always lose — it has zero expected growth, so it drifts nowhere while swinging violently, and individual paths land all over. The problem is the shape: a median below Kelly's, a fifth percentile near zero, and an upside that arrives too rarely to be a plan.
06

Why almost nobody bets full Kelly

Optimal growth comes with drawdowns most people cannot sit through, and the formula does not care how that feels.

Kelly maximizes long-run growth. It says nothing whatsoever about the route, and the route is brutal.

The classic result is that a full-Kelly bettor has, over a long enough horizon, a 50% chance of the bankroll halving at some point, and a 10% chance of it falling to a tenth. Over a finite thousand bets the simulation here shows the bankroll touching half its starting value 42% of the time — with a median finish of about 4× and a fifth percentile of 0.26×, which is to say a three-quarters loss.

Halving the stake changes the picture completely. At half Kelly the chance of ever halving drops to 9.5% over the same thousand bets — a quarter of full Kelly's rate — the fifth percentile rises from 0.26× to 0.72×, and you still keep three quarters of the growth. That trade — a quarter of the growth for most of the pain — is why practitioners talk about fractional Kelly and hardly anyone runs the full number.

There is a deeper reason too. Kelly assumes you know p and b exactly. You do not. Every source of estimation error pushes the honest fraction down, and half Kelly is a rough way of admitting that your edge estimate has a standard error too.

Try this

Set the fraction to a quarter of Kelly and predict what happens to the fifth percentile. Then check what it costs in median growth.

Check yourself

You are confident your edge is 55% but you know the estimate is noisy. What should that do to your stake?

Down, and the asymmetry from chapter 05 is why. If you are unsure whether the right fraction is 3% or 8%, betting 3% costs you a little growth and betting 8% can cost you everything. When the penalty for one error is mild and the other is ruinous, the point estimate is the wrong thing to bet — which is a general principle and not a fact about gambling.

Question 1 · chapter 01

An American roulette wheel pays 35 to 1 on a single number. What is the expected value per dollar?

−5.26 cents, and the whole edge is the two green pockets. The third answer is the European wheel, which has one zero instead of two and a house edge of exactly half as much — the same layout, the same payouts, and twice the value to the player.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Red-black and straight-up have the same house edge. How do their standard deviations compare?

5.8 times. Expectation and variance are independent facts about a bet, and a payoff table can hold one fixed while moving the other enormously. Almost everything that matters in practice — sizing, drawdown, how long an edge takes to show — depends on the second one.
Question 3 · chapter 03

A genuine 55% bettor at −110 has made 100 bets. What is the chance they are behind?

Roughly 30%. The noise is nineteen times the edge per bet, and averaging only divides it by √100 = 10. Judging a strategy on a hundred bets is judging it on a sample where the honest answer is still "no idea."
Question 4 · chapter 04

You win 55% of the time at even money. What does Kelly say to stake?

10%, and the shortcut is worth memorizing: at even money, Kelly is just the edge. A 55% shot at even money has a 10% edge and a 10% Kelly fraction. At −110 the same win rate is only worth 5.5%, because the worse price both shrinks the edge and makes each win repair less.
Question 5 · chapter 05

What is the long-run growth rate of a bettor staking exactly twice Kelly?

Exactly zero. Half Kelly, by contrast, keeps 74.9% of the growth. The curve is a hill and it is not symmetric — undershooting is nearly free and overshooting is not — which is the entire practical argument for fractional Kelly.
Question 6 · chapter 06

Why do professionals bet a fraction of Kelly rather than the full amount?

Estimation error and drawdown, in that order. The fourth answer is a real position some people hold — that maximizing log wealth is not everyone's objective — but it is a different argument. The everyday reason is simpler: you do not know p, and being wrong about p at full size is much more expensive than being conservative about it.

Kelly here assumes bets are made one at a time, that the edge and odds are known exactly, and that the bankroll is infinitely divisible with no minimum stake — all three are false in practice and all three push the honest fraction down. Simultaneous or correlated bets need a different calculation entirely: two bets on the same game are one bet with a bigger stake, and treating them as independent Kelly bets overstakes badly. The drawdown figures are simulated over a thousand bets with a fixed seed, so they are reproducible rather than typical; the asymptotic results quoted alongside them — a 50% chance of ever halving at full Kelly — apply to an infinite horizon and are worse than what a finite run shows. And every number on this page assumes the edge is real, which is the assumption module 08 exists to interrogate.
