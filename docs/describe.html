An interactive derivation · No. 04

One number to stand for all of them

Summarizing a pile of numbers means throwing almost all of them away. Which summary you pick decides what survives — and whether a single bad observation can drag the answer anywhere it likes.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Three questions get asked of every dataset: where is it, how spread out is it, and where does one particular value sit inside it. There is more than one answer to each, and the answers disagree exactly when it matters.

  1. Chapter 01 is the one to feel in your hands. Ten salaries with one founder in them have a mean of 167.9 and a median of 54.0. Push the founder's pay to 120,000 and the mean goes to 12,048 while the median does not move at all.
  2. Chapter 02 gives the conversions. For a normal distribution the three spread measures are locked together: the interquartile range is 1.349σ and the median absolute deviation is 0.6745σ. Outside a normal they come apart, and the gap is informative.
  3. Chapter 04 has the fact nobody mentions. The standard outlier rule flags 0.70% of perfectly well-behaved normal points — about one in every 143. It is a prompt to look, not a verdict.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
MeanThe arithmetic average. Uses every value, and is moved by every value.
MedianThe middle value once sorted. Uses only the rank, so distant values cannot pull it.
σStandard deviation — the root of the mean squared deviation.
IQRInterquartile range: the third quartile minus the first, holding the middle half.
MADMedian absolute deviation — the median of the distances from the median. The most stubbornly robust measure of spread there is.
PercentileThe fraction of the data at or below a value.
z-score(value − mean) ÷ σ. How many standard deviations from the middle, and unitless.
Breakdown pointThe fraction of the data that can be arbitrarily corrupted before a statistic becomes meaningless.
01

Where is it?

The mean uses every value, which is its strength and its whole vulnerability.

Ten salaries at a small company, in thousands: 42, 45, 48, 51, 53, 55, 58, 62, 65 — and the founder on 1,200.

The mean is 167.9. The median is 54.0. Nine of the ten people earn less than a third of the mean, so the mean describes nobody in the room. The median describes almost everyone.

Remove the founder and the mean falls to 53.2 — a move of 114.7. The median moves from 54.0 to 53.0, a move of 1.0. One observation out of ten controlled the mean and barely touched the median.

The formal version of that is the breakdown point: the share of the data that can be arbitrary garbage before the statistic becomes meaningless. For the mean it is 0%. Change a single value and I can move the mean anywhere I want. For the median it is 50% — you must corrupt half the dataset before it can be forced anywhere, and no statistic can do better than that.

Watch it happen: push the founder to 12,000 and the mean becomes 1,247.9; push to 120,000 and it becomes 12,047.9. The median reads 54.0 throughout, unmoved.

None of which makes the mean wrong. It is the right summary when you care about the total — a payroll department needs the mean, because the mean times headcount is the bill. It is the wrong summary when you want to describe a typical member, and the two get quoted interchangeably.

Check yourself

A report says average household wealth in a town is $2.4m and median wealth is $180k. What does that tell you?

A mean far above a median means a long right tail. The gap between the two is itself a measurement — it is the crudest skew detector there is, and it is free. When a summary quotes only one of them, which one was chosen is usually the most informative thing about the report.
02

How spread out is it?

Three answers, locked together under a normal and pulling apart everywhere else.

Spread has the same problem as center, and worse. The standard deviation squares every deviation before averaging, so a distant point counts for its distance squared — the vulnerability of the mean, amplified.

On those ten salaries the standard deviation is 362.7. Drop the founder and it is 7.6, smaller by a factor of 47. The interquartile range — the third quartile minus the first, covering the middle half — goes from 12.25 to 10.00, a factor of 1.2. The median absolute deviation goes from 7.0 to 5.0.

Under a normal distribution these are all the same quantity in different clothes, and the conversions are worth having:

The IQR is 1.349σ — exactly twice the 0.6745 that marks the quartiles. The median absolute deviation is 0.6745σ. And the mean absolute deviation is 0.7979σ, which is the constant an option straddle prices and a story for elsewhere.

So a robust volatility estimate is IQR × 0.7413 or MAD × 1.4826, and those constants exist purely to make the robust measures comparable with a standard deviation.

Here is the part worth internalizing. When those conversions disagree on real data, that is not an error — it is a measurement. A sample whose IQR-implied sigma is far below its actual standard deviation has fat tails, and the size of the gap is roughly how fat. You get a distributional diagnosis for free, from two numbers you were computing anyway.

IQR = 1.349 σ median abs deviation = 0.6745 σ mean abs deviation = 0.7979 σ

Reads as: for a normal, every measure of spread is the standard deviation times a fixed constant. The constants hold only for a normal, so when two of them disagree on real data you have learned the data is not normal.

Check yourself

A return series has a standard deviation of 2.0% but its IQR implies only 1.2%. What have you found?

The disagreement is the finding. Both statistics are computed correctly; the constant that relates them is what assumed normality. This is one of the cheapest fat-tail diagnostics available, and it needs nothing beyond two numbers you already have.
03

Where does this one sit?

A percentile counts what is below. A z-score measures in standard deviations. One is distribution-free and one is not.

Two ways to place a single value inside a distribution, and they answer subtly different questions.

A percentile is a rank: the 88th percentile means 88% of the data sits at or below you. It needs no assumptions at all — it is a count — and it is bounded between 0 and 100 whatever the shape.

A z-score is a distance: (value − mean) ÷ σ, measured in standard deviations. It carries more information when the distribution is well behaved and it is unbounded, which matters for the tails.

Under a normal they convert exactly. A z of 1.00 is the 84.13th percentile; 2.00 is the 97.72nd; 3.00 is the 99.87th. Read symmetrically, that is the empirical rule with its true values rather than its rounded ones: 68.27% within one sigma, 95.45% within two, 99.73% within three.

The z-score's real power is comparing things that are not otherwise comparable. A 620 on a test with mean 500 and σ 100 is z = 1.20, the 88.49th percentile. A 31 on a test with mean 21 and σ 5 is z = 2.00, the 97.72nd. The second is much the better result, and no amount of staring at 620 against 31 will tell you that.

But the conversion runs on the normal assumption and nothing else. On a fat-tailed distribution a z of 3 is nowhere near the 99.87th percentile — it is far more common than that — which is the whole subject of module 12. The percentile keeps meaning what it says; the z-score's translation into a probability is what breaks.

z = (value − mean) ÷ σ percentile = Φ(z), for a normal only

Reads as: subtract the middle and divide by the spread; then, if the distribution is normal, convert that to a fraction. The first step always works. The second is an assumption.

Try this

Set the value to three sigma and read the percentile. Then imagine the tail is twice as heavy and ask what that number would really be.

Check yourself

Which is more impressive: 620 on a test with mean 500 and σ 100, or 31 on one with mean 21 and σ 5?

The 31, by a wide margin. Standardizing is exactly what makes the fourth option wrong — different tests become comparable the moment you measure in standard deviations instead of raw points. That is most of what a z-score is for.
04

Five numbers, and a fence that cries wolf

The box plot is the five-number summary drawn. Its outlier rule flags one in every 143 perfectly good points.

The five-number summary is minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile, maximum. For the salaries that is 42, 48.75, 54.0, 61.0, 1200. Drawn as a box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median and whiskers reaching out, it is a box plot, and it shows center, spread and skew at once.

The whiskers stop at the 1.5 × IQR fences. Here the IQR is 12.25, so the fences sit at 30.4 and 79.4, and the 1,200 is plotted as a lone point outside — flagged.

Now the part that rarely gets said. That fence is not a test, and it has a false positive rate you can compute. For a normal distribution the quartiles sit at ±0.6745σ, so the upper fence sits at 0.6745 + 1.5 × 1.349 = 2.698σ. The chance a normal point lands outside is 0.6977%.

So in a hundred perfectly clean normal observations you should expect 0.70 points flagged as outliers, and in a thousand you should expect seven. The rule flags about one in every 143 good points. It was designed as a prompt to go and look, not as a verdict that something is wrong, and treating flagged points as errors to delete is how a dataset quietly loses its tails — which is precisely the part module 12 says carries the risk.

Where this comes from

For a normal, the quartiles are at ±0.67449σ — the points with 25% and 75% of the mass below them. So the IQR is 2 × 0.67449 = 1.34898σ, which is where the 1.349 conversion comes from.

The upper fence is Q3 + 1.5 × IQR, which in sigmas is 0.67449 + 1.5 × 1.34898 = 2.69796σ. The probability of a normal point beyond that on either side is 2 × (1 − Φ(2.698)) = 0.6977%, or roughly one point in 143.

Where it breaks: everything above assumes normality, and the rule is most often reached for precisely when the data is not normal. On a genuinely fat-tailed sample the fence fires far more often than 0.7% — not because anything is wrong, but because the distribution has more mass out there than a normal does. The 1.5 is also pure convention with no derivation behind it; Tukey chose it because it worked, and 3.0 is the conventional threshold for "far out." Reading either as a significance test imports a rigor that was never claimed.

Check yourself

A box plot of 1,000 clean, normally distributed observations. How many points fall outside the whiskers?

About seven, and none of them is an error. The rule fires at a fixed rate on well-behaved data, which is why flagged points are a prompt to look rather than a license to delete. Deleting them systematically trims exactly the tail that carries the risk.
05

What happens when you change the units

Adding a constant moves the center and leaves the spread alone. Multiplying moves both. That asymmetry is why z-scores work.

Take the nine salaries without the founder — mean 53.22, median 53.00, standard deviation 7.645, IQR 10.00.

Add 10 to everyone. The mean becomes 63.22 and the median 63.00, both shifted by exactly 10. The standard deviation is still 7.645 and the IQR still 10.00 — completely unmoved. Sliding a distribution sideways does not change how spread out it is.

Double everyone instead. The mean becomes 106.44 and the median 106.00, both doubled. But now the standard deviation doubles to 15.29 and the IQR to 20.00 as well. Stretching a distribution stretches its spread.

That asymmetry is the whole reason the z-score is built the way it is. Subtracting the mean removes the shift; dividing by σ removes the scale. What is left cannot depend on either.

Compute the z-scores of the original, of the shifted data, and of the doubled data, and all three come out identical to the last decimal place. The standardized data always has mean exactly 0 and standard deviation exactly 1, whatever units you started in — dollars, degrees, basis points. That is why chapter 03 could compare two different tests, and why every distribution in this series gets discussed in sigmas.

mean(aX + b) = a·mean(X) + b sd(aX + b) = |a|·sd(X)

Reads as: the center follows both the stretch and the shift; the spread follows only the stretch. The absolute value is there because flipping the sign reverses the data without making it less spread out.

Check yourself

Every salary in a company rises by exactly $5,000. What happens to the standard deviation?

Unchanged. Spread measures distances, and a uniform shift changes no distance. A 10% raise for everyone is a different story — that is a multiplication, and it scales the standard deviation by 1.10 along with the mean.

Question 2 · chapter 01

What is the breakdown point of the median?

Half, which is the theoretical maximum. The first option is the mean's breakdown point. And the fourth is not achievable by anything: once more than half the data is arbitrary, the "middle" is whatever the corrupted majority says it is.
Question 3 · chapter 02

For a normal distribution, what is the interquartile range in standard deviations?

1.349σ. The fourth option is the median absolute deviation, which is a quartile's distance rather than the range between two. Dividing an IQR by 1.349 gives a robust sigma estimate, and when that disagrees with the actual sigma you have found fat tails.
Question 4 · chapter 03

A value sits two standard deviations above the mean of a normal distribution. What percentile?

97.72nd. The 95 in the empirical rule is the fraction within ±2σ, not the fraction below +2σ — a one-tail versus two-tail slip that costs about three percentage points and shows up constantly.
Question 5 · chapter 04

How often does the 1.5 × IQR rule flag a perfectly normal observation?

Roughly one in 143. So a thousand clean points produce about seven flags, none of them errors. The rule is a prompt to go and look; treating flagged points as bad data trims exactly the tail that carries the risk.
Question 6 · chapter 05

You add 10 to every value in a dataset. What happens to the mean and the standard deviation?

Center shifts, spread does not. Multiplying is the other case — it scales both. Standardizing subtracts the mean and divides by σ precisely to remove both effects, which is why z-scores come out identical whatever units you started in.

Quartiles have no single agreed definition and different software gives slightly different answers on small samples; this page uses linear interpolation between order statistics, which is the most common convention and matches R's default type 7, but a spreadsheet may disagree in the second decimal on ten data points. The 1.349 and 0.6745 constants are exact for a normal distribution and for nothing else — applying them to visibly skewed data produces a number with no clear meaning, which is why chapter 02 treats their disagreement as a diagnostic rather than a nuisance. The standard deviation here divides by n − 1, the sample convention, which matters at these sample sizes and is invisible by a few hundred points. And the 1.5 in the outlier fence is a convention with no derivation behind it: Tukey picked it because it behaved well, and reading it as a significance test imports a rigor that was never claimed for it.
