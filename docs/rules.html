Four rules, and the two places everyone slips

The complete rule set fits on one page: complement, addition, multiplication, and conditioning. The rules are easy. Knowing which one you are in is not, and two specific confusions survive every course anyone takes.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Module 01 counted outcomes. This one combines them. Everything here is arithmetic you already know — the work is entirely in deciding which rule applies, and the page is built around the places that decision goes wrong.

  1. Chapter 01 is the most useful habit in the subject. When "at least one" appears, stop and count the opposite. A messy union of overlapping cases becomes a single product.
  2. Chapter 02 holds the first trap. Mutually exclusive is not independent — it is close to the opposite. Two events that cannot both happen are maximally informative about each other.
  3. Chapter 04 holds the second. A coin has no memory and a shrinking deck does. The gambler's fallacy is not simply wrong; it is right about decks and wrong about coins, and knowing which you are looking at is the whole skill.

01

Count what you don't want

"At least one" is a union of overlapping cases and a nightmare to count directly. Its opposite is a single product.

The first rule is the one that sounds too simple to be worth naming: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). It earns its place because of when to reach for it.

Roll a die four times. What is the chance of at least one six? Counted directly it is a mess — a six on the first roll, or the second, or both, and the overlaps have to be stripped out so nothing is double-counted. Counted the other way it is one line: every roll must miss, which is (5/6)4, so the answer is 1 − (5/6)4 = 51.77%.

Any time you see at least one, that is the move. It converts a union into a product, and products are easy.

This exact question is where probability theory started. The Chevalier de Méré made money betting on at least one six in four rolls of one die, then reasoned that at least one double-six in twenty-four rolls of two dice should be the same bet — twenty-four is to thirty-six as four is to six. It is not. The first is 51.77% and the second is 49.14%, and he lost steadily until he asked Pascal why.

The two sit on opposite sides of a half by about two and a half points. That is a small enough gap to be invisible to intuition and large enough to bankrupt someone who bets on it repeatedly, which is as good a summary of this whole series as anything.

P(at least one in n) = 1 − (1 − p)n

Reads as: the chance everything misses, subtracted from one. The right-hand side is a product because the misses are independent; the left-hand side would be a union, and unions need every overlap accounted for.

Find the number of attempts at which a one-in-a-hundred event becomes more likely than not. Guess before you drag.

Check yourself

Why compute "at least one six" through its complement?

A union becomes a product. Both routes give the same answer; one of them is four lines of inclusion-exclusion and the other is a single power. The fourth option is what makes the complement a product rather than something worse, but it is not the reason to prefer the complement.
02

Adding, and the overlap you counted twice

P(A or B) is not P(A) + P(B) unless they cannot both happen. And "cannot both happen" is not what independent means.

Draw one card. What is the chance it is a heart or a face card? Thirteen hearts, twelve face cards, so twenty-five out of fifty-two? No — because three cards are both, and you just counted each of them twice.

The rule corrects for exactly that: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). Here that is 13 + 12 − 3 = 22 cards, or 42.31%, against the naive 48.08%. The gap is the three cards, 5.77%.

When the two cannot happen together the correction is zero and the rule collapses to plain addition. A card cannot be a heart and a spade, so P(heart or spade) = 13/52 + 13/52 = 50% with nothing to subtract. Events like that are mutually exclusive.

Now the trap, and it is worth slowing down for. Mutually exclusive is not independent. People treat the two words as near-synonyms and they are closer to opposites.

Heart and spade are mutually exclusive: P(both) = 0. If they were independent, P(both) would be 0.25 × 0.25 = 6.25%. Independence would mean knowing it is a heart tells you nothing about whether it is a spade. In fact it tells you everything — it rules a spade out completely. Mutually exclusive events are about as dependent as two events can be.

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

Reads as: add both, then remove the part you counted twice. If nothing was counted twice the last term is zero, and that is the only case where plain addition is correct.

Check yourself

Two events are mutually exclusive. Are they independent?

They are maximally dependent. The only way to be both mutually exclusive and independent is for one of them to have probability zero. Two events with real probabilities that cannot co-occur are as informative about each other as events get — which is why the two terms should never be used interchangeably.
03

Multiplying, and what the second term is doing

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B given A). Independence is the special case where the second term stops caring about the first.

Two aces from a shuffled deck. The first card is an ace with probability 4/52. The second is an ace with probability 3/51 — given the first one was, because the deck has changed.

So P(both) = (4/52) × (3/51) = 0.4525%, about 1 in 221. And it agrees with module 01's counting: C(4,2)/C(52,2) = 6/1326, the same number by a different route.

Had you replaced and reshuffled between draws, the events would be independent and the answer would be (4/52)2 = 0.5917% — larger, because the deck never shrank. Independence is not a separate rule; it is the case where P(B | A) = P(B) and the general rule simplifies.

One consequence is worth stating on its own because it is violated constantly in ordinary reasoning: P(A and B) can never exceed P(A) or P(B). Adding detail to a story can only make it less probable, never more. Six independent conditions of 90% each leave 53.1%, and a forecast with a dozen specific parts is almost certainly wrong even when every part is individually likely.

P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B | A) and if independent, = P(A) · P(B)

Reads as: the chance of the first, times the chance of the second once the first has happened. Independence is not an extra rule — it is what makes the second factor stop depending on the first.

Check yourself

Which is more probable: that a company beats earnings, or that it beats earnings and the stock rallies?

The first, with no exceptions. P(A and B) ≤ P(A) is arithmetic, not a tendency, and correlation does not rescue the fourth option — it can push P(B given A) close to one, but never above it. A detailed story feeling more likely than a vague one is the best-documented failure in the whole field.
04

A coin has no memory. A deck does.

The gambler's fallacy is not simply an error. It is correct about one of these and wrong about the other.

Five heads in a row. Is the next flip more likely to be tails? No. The coin has no mechanism for remembering, and P(head | five heads) = 0.5 exactly as before.

What makes this genuinely confusing is that a true statement sits right next to the false one. Before any flips, six heads in a row has probability (1/2)6 = 1.56% — genuinely unlikely. After five heads have landed, the sixth is a coin flip. Both are correct. They are different questions asked at different moments, and the fallacy is answering the first when you are standing at the second.

Now the part usually left out. The intuition behind the fallacy — that things even out — is right about a great many real situations. It is right about anything drawn without replacement.

Deal cards from a deck. After twenty-six black cards have gone, the next card is red with probability 100%. Ten black cards in gives 61.90%; twenty gives 81.25%. The deck really does even out, because every card removed changes what is left. That is what card counting is, and it is why it works.

So the skill is not "never expect things to even out." It is asking whether the thing you are watching has a memory. A deck does. A roulette wheel does not. A market is somewhere in between and the answer depends entirely on the mechanism, which is why module 09 is careful about the difference between a pattern and a reason.

Check yourself

A roulette wheel has come up red eight times. Is black now more likely?

Nothing changed. Eight reds in a row is unlikely in advance; conditional on having happened, the next spin is what it always was. The fourth option is the opposite error — one run is far too little evidence for bias, which is what module 09 is about. And the intuition is not worthless: apply it to a deck instead of a wheel and it is exactly right.
05

Conditioning is just restricting the world

P(B | A) means: throw away every outcome where A did not happen, then ask about B in what remains.

Conditional probability has a formula — P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A) — but the formula is easier to use than to trust. The picture is better: cross out every outcome where A failed, then measure B against the survivors.

A family has two children and at least one is a boy. What is the chance both are? Four equally likely families: BB, BG, GB, GG. "At least one boy" removes GG, leaving three. One of those three is BB, so the answer is 1/3 — not the 1/2 nearly everyone says.

Change one word and the answer changes. "The elder is a boy" removes both GG and GB, leaving BB and BG, so the answer is 1/2. The two conditions sound almost identical and select different sets. That is the entire content of conditioning, and it is why the wording of a condition matters more than the arithmetic.

The same idea settles Monty Hall. Three doors, you pick one, the host — who knows where the car is — opens a different door showing a goat. Switching wins 2/3 of the time and staying wins 1/3.

It feels like two doors should be even money, but the host's move is not independent of where the car is. He is constrained to avoid it, so the door he leaves closed carries the two-thirds that was never yours. Condition on what the host could have done, not just on the door count, and the answer follows.

P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A)

Reads as: of the world where A happened, what fraction also has B? The denominator is the shrunken world, which is why conditioning can move a probability in either direction.

Where this comes from

Monty Hall is the cleanest case to do properly. Your first pick is right with probability 1/3 and wrong with probability 2/3. If you picked right, the host may open either remaining door and switching loses. If you picked wrong — two times in three — the host has no choice: one remaining door hides the car and he must open the other. So in every one of those cases the closed door is the car, and switching wins.

Switching wins exactly when your first pick was wrong, which is 2/3. Simulating four hundred thousand games gives 0.3339 for staying and 0.6661 for switching, against the exact 1/3 and 2/3.

Where it breaks: the whole result rests on the host knowing where the car is and being obliged to open a goat. If he opens a door at random and it happens to show a goat, switching wins only 1/2 — the conditioning is different because a random host could have revealed the car and did not, which is information the constrained host never supplies. Change the host's rule and you change the answer, which is exactly the point: the condition is not "a door was opened", it is "a door was opened by someone following this rule."

Check yourself

Two children, at least one is a boy. Chance both are boys?

1/3, and 1/2 is the answer to a different question. "The elder is a boy" removes GG and GB, leaving two outcomes and giving 1/2. Two conditions that sound the same select different sets — which is why conditioning goes wrong at the wording rather than at the arithmetic.

Four rolls of a die. Chance of at least one six?

51.77%. The fourth option is de Méré's other bet — at least one double-six in 24 rolls of two dice, at 49.14%. He assumed the two were the same and lost money on the difference, which is how Pascal came to work on any of this.
Question 2 · chapter 02

One card. P(heart or face card)?

22/52. The first option is the naive sum at 48.08% — wrong by exactly the three cards that are both, 5.77%. Plain addition is correct only when the overlap is empty.
Question 3 · chapter 02

Two events cannot both happen. What does that make them?

As dependent as events get. Heart and spade have P(both) = 0 where independence would require 6.25%. The only way to be both mutually exclusive and independent is for one of them to be impossible.
Question 4 · chapter 03

Two cards from a deck. P(both aces)?

1 in 221. The first option assumes replacement and comes out too high, because it lets the deck keep all four aces. Counting instead of multiplying gives the same answer: C(4,2)/C(52,2) = 6/1326.
Question 5 · chapter 04

Five heads in a row on a fair coin. P(the sixth is a head)?

1/2, unchanged. The third option answers the different question — the chance of six in a row from a standing start. And the intuition behind the first is not worthless: apply it to a deck, where cards are removed, and evening out is exactly what happens.
Question 6 · chapter 05

Monty Hall. You pick a door, the host opens a goat, you switch. P(win)?

2/3, and it is the host's constraint that does it. He knows where the car is and must open a goat, so when your first pick was wrong — two thirds of the time — he has no choice and the closed door is the car. If he opened a door at random and it happened to show a goat, switching would win only 1/2.

The two-children puzzle depends on how the family was selected, and the version here assumes you sampled families uniformly and then learned that at least one child is a boy. Learning it a different way — meeting one child at random and seeing a boy — gives 1/2 rather than 1/3, and neither answer is wrong; they answer different questions about different sampling procedures. The same caveat runs through Monty Hall, where the host's rule is the whole problem: knowing the car's location and being obliged to reveal a goat gives 2/3, while a host opening at random gives 1/2. Both puzzles are famous because the English sentence describing them is ambiguous in a way the mathematics is not. The card examples assume a fair shuffle and a standard 52-card deck, and the coin examples assume a genuinely fair coin — a real coin flipped by a real person is very slightly biased toward the face it started on, by about half a percentage point, which is far too small to matter here and far too small to detect without the sample sizes of module 09.
